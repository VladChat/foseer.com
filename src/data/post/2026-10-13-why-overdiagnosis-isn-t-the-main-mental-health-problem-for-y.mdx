Young people are not being harmed by a flood of spurious mental health labels, but by the failure to provide timely, effective care. That is the central argument of a recent Guardian editorial, which contends that “overdiagnosis” is being overstated while the core problems of access, quality and investment in mental health services remain unresolved.

The editorial, published by the Guardian, frames the current debate as a distraction from the more basic reality: for many young people, appropriate support is still out of reach, and parity of esteem between mental and physical healthcare remains a long way off.

## The Guardian’s central argument

The Guardian’s editorial board sets out its position in response to growing public discussion about whether mental health conditions are being overdiagnosed in younger generations. The piece argues that this focus risks misrepresenting the situation facing children, teenagers and young adults.

According to the Guardian, the evidence points to significant unmet need rather than an epidemic of inappropriate diagnosis. The editorial notes that young people are waiting long periods for assessment and treatment, and that many who seek help struggle to access specialist services at all. Within that context, the board argues, concerns about overdiagnosis can obscure the more pressing issue: a system that does not yet meet demand.

The paper’s view is that mental health problems among young people are real, serious and often under-addressed. Labelling the situation as one of overdiagnosis, it suggests, risks implying that young people are being medicalised unnecessarily, when the more common experience is difficulty in obtaining recognition and care.

## Parity with physical healthcare “a distant prospect”

A key line in the Guardian editorial is its assessment that parity of esteem between mental and physical health services remains “a distant prospect.” Parity of esteem is a policy goal under which mental health is supposed to be valued and resourced on a level comparable to physical health.

In practice, the Guardian argues, this goal has not been realised. The editorial points to persistent gaps in provision and long waits for mental health care as evidence that the system has not caught up with the scale of need. By contrast, physical health problems are more likely to be met with clear pathways, established services and expectations of timely treatment.

The board’s view is that, until mental health services are funded and organised in a way that reflects their importance, debates about whether too many diagnoses are being made risk missing the structural shortfall. The editorial uses the phrase “distant prospect” to underline how far current arrangements fall short of the parity that has been promised.

## Why the focus on overdiagnosis matters

The Guardian editorial treats the overdiagnosis debate as more than a semantic dispute. It argues that framing young people’s difficulties as a problem of excessive diagnosis can have practical consequences.

One concern, as set out in the piece, is that the overdiagnosis narrative may feed scepticism about young people’s reports of distress. If public discussion leans heavily on the idea that too many young people are being given labels, those seeking help may feel their experiences are being minimised or dismissed.

The editorial also suggests that policy responses could be affected. If the dominant story is that services are too quick to diagnose, the perceived solution might be to tighten thresholds or reduce referrals, rather than to expand access and improve care. The Guardian’s board positions itself firmly against that direction, arguing that the weight of current evidence points to under-provision, not over-supply.

## A call to refocus on services and support

Throughout the editorial, the Guardian returns to the theme of services that do not yet match the needs of young people. While the piece acknowledges the public conversation about overdiagnosis, it consistently redirects attention to the availability and quality of care.

The board highlights that mental health problems in childhood and adolescence can have long-term consequences if not addressed early. Within that frame, delays in assessment or treatment are presented as a serious policy failure. The editorial suggests that investment, staffing and service design all require sustained attention if outcomes for young people are to improve.

Rather than questioning whether too many diagnoses are being made, the Guardian’s position is that the more urgent questions are whether young people can get help when they need it, whether that help is evidence-based and appropriate, and whether mental health is being treated with the same seriousness as physical illness.

## Why this debate matters

The Guardian’s intervention is significant because it challenges a narrative that has gained visibility in public and media discussion. By arguing that overdiagnosis is not the central problem for young people, the editorial seeks to reframe how policymakers, professionals and the public think about mental health provision.

At stake are decisions about where limited resources are directed and how services respond when young people ask for help. If the Guardian’s analysis is correct, the main risk is not that too many young people are being drawn into mental health services, but that too many are being left without effective support.

The editorial leaves readers with a clear message: until mental health services for young people are resourced and prioritised on a level comparable with physical healthcare, arguments about overdiagnosis will remain secondary to the more basic challenge of making sure those who need help can get it.
