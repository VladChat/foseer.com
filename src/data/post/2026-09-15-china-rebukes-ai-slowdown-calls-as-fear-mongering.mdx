China’s government has publicly pushed back against prominent Western artificial intelligence executives who have urged a slowdown in advanced AI development, describing those calls as “fear mongering,” according to reporting from CNBC. The remarks, delivered at a regular state press briefing and relayed by Chinese state media, underscore how differently Beijing and some US‑based tech leaders now frame the risks and opportunities of powerful AI systems.

State media accounts of the briefing also emphasized that all parties should work together to keep AI “open” and “inclusive,” signaling that China wants to be seen as a champion of broad access rather than tighter brakes on development.

## What China is rejecting when it says ‘slowdown’

CNBC reports that Chinese officials were responding to recent public appeals by high‑profile AI executives who have warned that rapid, unregulated advances in so‑called frontier AI systems—very large, general‑purpose models—could pose serious societal and security risks. Those executives have floated ideas ranging from voluntary pauses on training more powerful models to formal international agreements to slow certain kinds of development.

According to the CNBC account, China’s response framed these slowdown proposals not as prudent risk management but as attempts to stoke anxiety. By labeling the calls “fear mongering,” Beijing is effectively arguing that warnings about runaway AI risk are exaggerated, politicized, or both.

A separate analysis from the Atlantic Council, which examines the slowdown debate more broadly, notes that many of these proposals originate in US and European policy circles and from executives at leading Western AI firms. That context helps explain why Chinese officials might see the rhetoric around slowing AI as intertwined with geopolitical competition, even if they do not say so explicitly in the press briefing.

## How Beijing’s message contrasts with AI leaders’ warnings

The core disagreement is not over whether AI has risks—both sides acknowledge that—but over what to do about the pace of development.

Western AI executives who have advocated a slowdown, as summarized in the Atlantic Council’s overview, typically argue that:

- Current safety techniques may not scale reliably to much more capable systems.
- Time is needed to build stronger safeguards, testing regimes, and governance structures.
- Moving too fast could create hard‑to‑reverse dependencies on systems that are not fully understood.

By contrast, China’s press‑briefing line, as reported by CNBC, suggests that officials see talk of slowing down as at best premature and at worst a tactic that could constrain progress. The use of the term “fear mongering” implies that, in Beijing’s view, such warnings may unduly alarm the public or policymakers and could be used to justify restrictions that limit who benefits from AI.

The same briefing, according to state media accounts cited by CNBC, paired this criticism with a call for AI that is “open” and “inclusive.” In practice, that framing points toward:

- Continued rapid development of AI systems.
- Emphasis on broad participation in AI research and deployment.
- Wariness of rules that might lock in advantages for early leaders.

## Why the ‘openness and inclusivity’ language matters

The language of openness and inclusivity is not accidental. It positions China as favoring shared benefits and access, in contrast to what officials portray as restrictive or alarmist approaches.

CNBC’s reporting on the briefing notes that Chinese state media highlighted this theme alongside the “fear mongering” remark. That pairing sends a dual message:

1. **Normative framing:** China is casting itself as supportive of collaborative, global AI development rather than unilateral constraints.
2. **Regulatory signaling:** Beijing is indicating that it prefers governance approaches that facilitate continued growth and diffusion of AI capabilities, rather than hard caps or moratoria.

The Atlantic Council’s contextual analysis points out that debates over AI slowdown often double as debates over who gets to set global standards. By emphasizing openness, China is signaling that it does not want to be bound by norms or agreements that it sees as originating primarily in Western risk narratives.

## What is at stake for key stakeholders

Although the current development is limited to statements at a press conference, the stance described by CNBC has practical implications for several groups.

### Chinese policymakers

For Chinese officials, rejecting a slowdown narrative helps preserve policy space to continue investing in and deploying advanced AI systems. It also allows them to present domestic regulations—such as content controls or security reviews—as compatible with, rather than contradictory to, a broader commitment to open and inclusive development.

The Atlantic Council’s overview of the slowdown debate suggests that governments weighing these issues must balance innovation, security, and economic competitiveness. China’s choice of language indicates that, at least for now, it is prioritizing momentum and access over formal commitments to slow frontier development.

### Western AI companies and executives

For the AI executives who have advocated a slowdown, China’s dismissal complicates any push for globally coordinated measures. If one of the world’s largest AI powers publicly characterizes slowdown calls as “fear mongering,” it becomes harder to argue that such measures reflect an emerging consensus.

The Atlantic Council analysis notes that many slowdown proposals assume some level of cooperation among leading AI jurisdictions. China’s reaction, as described by CNBC, highlights how fragile that assumption may be.

### International policy and research communities

Policy researchers and international organizations studying AI governance now face a more polarized rhetorical environment. On one side are voices emphasizing existential or systemic risks and arguing for slower development of the most powerful systems; on the other is a major state actor framing those arguments as alarmist.

This divergence, visible in the contrast between the slowdown narratives summarized by the Atlantic Council and the Chinese government’s remarks reported by CNBC, raises practical questions about how far any shared guardrails can extend.

## How likely is formal confirmation of China’s stance?

The reader question is whether China’s characterization of the slowdown calls as “fear mongering” is likely to be formally confirmed in the next week.

Two independent sources—CNBC’s event‑focused reporting and the Atlantic Council’s contextual analysis—describe the same underlying development: Chinese officials publicly rejecting AI slowdown appeals and using language that portrays them as driven by fear. CNBC’s account, based on state media coverage of the daily press conference, already reflects an official setting and message distribution.

In practice, there are a few ways this stance could be further confirmed:

- Additional written statements or transcripts from the same ministry.
- Repetition of the same framing at subsequent briefings.
- References to the “fear mongering” characterization in other official outlets.

Given that state media have already reported the remarks from an on‑the‑record briefing, and that two separate sources across two domains are aligned on the core description, it is reasonable to expect that this framing is not a one‑off misquote. Over the next week, the most likely pattern is reinforcement rather than reversal—either through repetition of similar language or through continued emphasis on openness and inclusivity as the preferred alternative to a slowdown.

There is still uncertainty about the exact wording future statements might use, and about whether the phrase “fear mongering” itself will be repeated verbatim. But the underlying position—skepticism toward calls for a slowdown and preference for continued rapid, broadly accessible AI development—now appears clearly established in the public record described by CNBC.

## What to watch in the coming weeks

Over the next several weeks to months, three plausible trajectories emerge from the evidence in the CNBC and Atlantic Council reports:

1. **Steady repetition of the current line.** Chinese officials may continue to describe slowdown proposals in negative terms while promoting openness and inclusivity. Indicators would include similar language at future press briefings and consistent messaging in state media.

2. **Selective engagement with international talks.** China could participate in discussions on AI safety and governance while quietly resisting any provisions that resemble a formal slowdown. In this scenario, the “fear mongering” label functions as a boundary marker: Beijing engages on safety but rejects caps on development speed.

3. **Issue‑specific clarification.** If future incidents or proposals sharpen the slowdown debate, Chinese authorities might refine their public stance—distinguishing, for example, between opposing broad pauses and supporting targeted restrictions in narrow areas. Any such nuance would likely show up first in detailed statements or policy documents rather than in brief press‑conference sound bites.

Across all scenarios, the key questions remain how far major AI powers are prepared to go in constraining frontier development, and whether any shared language can bridge the gap between those calling for a slowdown and those, like China in the CNBC account, who see that approach as driven by fear rather than necessity.
