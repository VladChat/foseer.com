Children’s books are often remembered less for their plots than for a single image: a fox in a forest, a furious grandmother, a bear who cannot find his hat. As reported by the Guardian in a feature on contemporary children’s illustrators, that visual memory is not accidental. Artists such as Quentin Blake, Cressida Cowell, Axel Scheffler and Lauren Child describe illustration as a deliberate craft of “being serious about being silly” – using humour, exaggeration and visual rhythm to pull children into the mechanics of storytelling.

The same Guardian report notes that this craft is about to gain a permanent home. Next month, the Quentin Blake Centre for Illustration is due to open in a converted 17th‑century industrial complex in London’s Clerkenwell, billed there as the largest institution of its kind. Together, the illustrators’ reflections and the new centre’s scale point to a shift: children’s illustration is being treated less as decoration and more as a distinct narrative art form.

## A new home for illustration – and what it signals

According to the Guardian, the Quentin Blake Centre for Illustration will occupy a sprawling former industrial site in Clerkenwell and is described as the largest dedicated illustration institution anywhere. The centre is named for Quentin Blake, best known for his work with author Roald Dahl on books such as *The Twits*.

The Guardian’s account positions the centre not just as a museum but as a place where illustration – especially for children – is studied and exhibited on its own terms. The scale and ambition, as described, suggest an attempt to move illustration from the margins of publishing into a more formal cultural role, closer to how galleries treat painting or sculpture.

If that framing holds, the centre effectively acknowledges what working illustrators in the Guardian piece argue: that pictures in children’s books are a primary storytelling tool, not an afterthought.

## “Serious about being silly”: how illustrators talk about their craft

The headline phrase quoted by the Guardian – “I am very serious about being silly” – captures a recurring theme in the illustrators’ comments: silliness is used with precision.

In the Guardian’s reporting, illustrators describe:

- **Silliness as structure** – Outlandish characters and visual jokes are not just for laughs; they guide a child’s eye through the page and mark key story beats.
- **Exaggeration as clarity** – Oversized noses, wild hair or impossible poses make emotions legible to early readers who may not yet decode complex text.
- **Play as invitation** – A deliberately chaotic or funny image signals that the book is a safe space to explore fear, anger or confusion at a manageable distance.

Blake’s own work, as discussed in the Guardian piece, shows this logic clearly. Spiky ink lines, skewed perspectives and slightly anarchic scenes create a sense that the story might leap off the page. That energy is not random; it is a visual promise that something will happen, which keeps children turning pages.

## How pictures teach children what a story is

The Guardian feature links illustrations from books such as *The Twits*, *The Gruffalo* and *I Want My Hat Back* to a shared goal: helping children understand how stories are built.

From that reporting, several patterns emerge:

### Character design as a narrative engine

In *The Gruffalo*, illustrated by Axel Scheffler, the creature’s mismatched features – “terrible tusks and terrible claws” – are drawn in a way that is both alarming and oddly endearing. As described in the Guardian piece, that balance lets children hold two ideas at once: the Gruffalo is scary, but he is also someone you can look at closely.

This kind of design choice does narrative work. It sets up tension (is he dangerous or not?) that the story then resolves. The illustration trains children to expect that characters can be complicated and that stories will answer the questions pictures raise.

### Repetition and rhythm on the page

The Guardian report notes how illustrators use recurring visual motifs – a fox’s footprints, a bear’s distinctive hat, the repeated posture of a sulking child – to create rhythm. That rhythm mirrors the repeated phrases in the text of many picture books.

For a child, this repetition is a cue: stories have patterns. When the same image returns with a small change, it signals that something in the story has shifted. The picture becomes a kind of visual grammar, teaching concepts like “before and after” or “cause and effect” without naming them.

### Visual gaps that invite participation

In Jon Klassen’s *I Want My Hat Back*, referenced in the Guardian feature through the image of an angry bear hunting for his hat, much of the story’s humour comes from what is not shown directly. The bear’s expression barely changes, but tiny shifts in his eyes and posture carry the joke.

The Guardian’s account highlights how illustrators rely on children to fill in these gaps. When the image does not spell everything out, young readers infer motives and emotions. That act of inference is central to understanding any narrative, and illustrators build it into their work from the start.

## Why illustrators say pictures are not “just” decoration

The Guardian feature gives several illustrators space to explain why they see themselves as storytellers, not simply as artists adding colour to someone else’s words.

From their comments, three linked ideas stand out:

- **Pictures control pacing.** A busy spread with many small details slows reading down as children point and ask questions. A stark, nearly empty page speeds things up or creates suspense. Illustrators interviewed by the Guardian describe planning this pacing deliberately.
- **Pictures manage emotion.** A frightening scene can be softened with warm colours or a slightly ridiculous facial expression. Conversely, a simple image can be made unsettling with sharp angles or heavy shadows. The Guardian’s reporting shows illustrators thinking carefully about how far to push fear or sadness for their audience.
- **Pictures support different kinds of readers.** For children who cannot yet read fluently, or who read in a second language, images carry much of the story. The Guardian piece underscores that illustrators are aware of this and design pages that can be “read” without decoding every word.

Taken together, these points support the illustrators’ argument, as relayed by the Guardian: that their work shapes not only how children feel about a story, but whether they understand it at all.

## The Quentin Blake Centre as a statement about value

The Guardian’s description of the new Quentin Blake Centre for Illustration – large, permanent, and devoted specifically to illustration – functions as more than a venue announcement. It signals that the kind of work Blake and his peers describe is being treated as culturally significant.

By locating the centre in a historic industrial complex, the project, as portrayed in the Guardian report, also nods to illustration’s status as both art and industry. Children’s books are commercial products, but the illustrators interviewed emphasise craft decisions that go beyond market trends: how to stage a joke over three pages, how to make a villain frightening but not nightmare‑inducing, how to keep a character recognisable from any angle.

Housing exhibitions, archives or educational programmes in one of the largest spaces of its kind – as the Guardian article characterises it – effectively formalises those concerns. It offers a place where the public can see sketches, drafts and alternative versions that usually remain invisible behind the finished book.

## What this means for how we think about children’s books

The Guardian feature and the forthcoming centre together suggest a quiet reframing of children’s literature.

Instead of treating picture books as a simple prelude to “real reading,” the illustrators’ comments, as reported, present them as sophisticated narrative tools. Silliness is handled with precision, character design carries plot, and page layouts teach children how to follow a story.

The Quentin Blake Centre, described by the Guardian as the largest dedicated illustration institution of its kind, reinforces that view by giving this work a dedicated, physical presence. It makes visible the argument that the pictures children fall in love with are not just charming extras, but the first maps they use to navigate stories at all.

For parents, teachers and readers, the message running through the Guardian’s coverage is straightforward: when a child lingers over a drawing of a grumpy giant or a worried bear, they are not only being entertained. They are practicing how to read the world – line by line, page by page, picture by picture.
