Artificial intelligence is no longer just a tool for defending computer networks. It is increasingly being used to discover weaknesses in those systems as well, including in other AI models.

In 2026, that shift is becoming more visible. Reporting highlighted by Gulf News describes how researchers are now deploying advanced AI systems, including Anthropic’s Claude, to probe OpenAI’s technology for vulnerabilities. The work illustrates a broader turn in cybersecurity: attacks and defenses are both starting to operate at the speed and scale of machines rather than humans.

While public details remain limited, coverage across Gulf News, Deutsche Welle (DW) and TechRadar consistently points to the same development: artificial intelligence is being used directly as a hacking instrument, not just as a background helper. All three outlets repeatedly link this trend to cybersecurity and intelligence work, and name OpenAI as a central actor in the unfolding story.

## What’s new: AI systems probing AI defenses

Gulf News, which focuses directly on the recent development, reports that security researchers have begun using AI models like Claude to test OpenAI’s systems. In practice, this means feeding prompts to OpenAI models, analyzing their responses, and systematically searching for ways to bypass safety rules or trigger unintended behavior.

Traditionally, this kind of “red teaming” — deliberately attacking a system to find weaknesses — has been done by human experts working manually or with custom scripts. According to the Gulf News account, the key change is that much of the probing can now be automated and scaled up through another AI system.

The reporting does not specify which OpenAI products are being tested or what concrete flaws, if any, have been discovered. It does, however, frame the exercise as part of a broader shift: AI models are now sophisticated enough to act as both the lock and the lockpick.

DW’s broader coverage of artificial intelligence, which the outlet groups under a dedicated AI topic area, supports this framing. DW notes that AI is increasingly embedded in critical digital infrastructure, from cloud services to consumer applications. That context helps explain why researchers are motivated to stress-test systems from major providers such as OpenAI.

## How AI changes the speed and scale of hacking

TechRadar, in its analysis of AI and cybersecurity, describes the current moment as a “red line” for the field. The outlet reports that AI-driven tools can generate and adapt cyberattacks much faster than human operators, raising concerns among security professionals.

Applied to the case highlighted by Gulf News, this means that when researchers use an AI model like Claude to probe OpenAI’s systems, they can:

- Generate large numbers of test prompts in rapid succession
- Systematically vary those prompts to search for weak spots
- Learn from previous responses and refine the next wave of tests

This feedback loop is a core feature of modern machine learning. Instead of a human manually crafting each attempt, the AI can help design and prioritize the most promising attack strategies within the rules set by its operators.

TechRadar’s reporting emphasizes that this approach does not just increase the volume of tests. It also helps uncover non-obvious interactions — for example, unusual combinations of instructions that might cause an AI model to ignore its own safety filters. While the public reporting does not detail specific exploits against OpenAI systems, it makes clear that this style of machine-assisted probing is now part of mainstream security practice.

## Why OpenAI is at the center of the story

OpenAI appears repeatedly across the coverage because of its role as a leading AI provider. Gulf News identifies OpenAI as the target of the Claude-based probing, while DW and TechRadar cite the company in broader discussions of AI’s security implications.

The focus on OpenAI matters for two reasons:

1. **Scale of deployment.** OpenAI models are integrated into a wide range of products and services. Any vulnerability in those systems could ripple across many organizations that rely on them.
2. **Influence on industry norms.** As one of the most visible AI companies, OpenAI’s approach to security testing and response is likely to shape expectations for the rest of the sector.

The available reporting does not indicate that OpenAI has suffered a public breach or major compromise as a result of the recent probing. Instead, the activity is presented as part of ongoing security research — an attempt to find and fix weaknesses before they can be exploited by malicious actors.

However, TechRadar’s coverage underscores that the same techniques used by researchers could, in principle, be repurposed by attackers. That dual-use nature is what makes the development significant. The tools that help secure OpenAI’s systems today could be used to attack other targets tomorrow.

## A new balance between offense and defense

Across Gulf News, DW, and TechRadar, one theme recurs: artificial intelligence is reshaping the balance between cyber offense and defense.

On the defensive side, AI can:

- Monitor large volumes of network traffic for anomalies
- Flag suspicious behavior faster than human analysts
- Help organizations prioritize which alerts to investigate

On the offensive side, AI can:

- Automate the search for software and configuration weaknesses
- Generate more convincing phishing messages and social engineering attempts
- Adapt attacks in real time based on the target’s responses

The Claude–OpenAI example reported by Gulf News sits at the intersection of these trends. Researchers are effectively using one AI system to simulate how an attacker might behave against another AI system, in order to strengthen defenses.

DW’s broader AI coverage notes that intelligence and security agencies are also watching these developments closely. While DW does not tie specific agencies to the Claude–OpenAI tests, it situates AI-enabled cybersecurity within a wider landscape of digital espionage and state-level competition.

The public reporting leaves open questions. It does not specify who exactly is running the Claude-based probes against OpenAI, whether they are affiliated with OpenAI, Anthropic, independent labs, or academic institutions. It also does not describe in detail how OpenAI is responding to any issues uncovered. Those gaps mean that some operational aspects of the story remain uncertain.

What is clear from the overlapping coverage is that using AI to hack — and to defend against hacking — is no longer hypothetical. It is part of current practice in 2026.

## What this means for users and organizations

For everyday users and organizations relying on AI services, the immediate implications are mostly indirect but important.

First, the fact that researchers are actively probing OpenAI’s systems with other AI models suggests that security testing is keeping pace with deployment, at least among leading providers. Gulf News presents the activity as a form of stress-testing rather than as a successful attack.

Second, the reporting from TechRadar highlights that the same capabilities can lower the barrier to entry for less-skilled attackers. Tools that automatically generate exploit attempts or craft targeted messages could spread beyond professional security teams.

Third, DW’s coverage of AI more broadly points to a likely increase in regulatory and policy attention on AI security. Although the sources do not detail specific new rules tied to this event, they indicate that policymakers are increasingly concerned about how AI interacts with critical digital infrastructure.

For now, the main practical takeaway is that organizations using AI tools should treat them like any other critical software dependency: subject to ongoing security review, configuration checks, and incident response planning.

## What to watch next

In the coming weeks, several developments could clarify how significant this moment is for cybersecurity.

Observers can watch for whether OpenAI or Anthropic publicly describe new security-testing programs or partnerships. Any official acknowledgment of AI-on-AI testing, or disclosure of vulnerabilities found through such methods, would provide more detail on how these systems are being hardened.

Security conferences and industry forums scheduled over the near term are also likely to feature discussions of AI-driven red teaming. If the techniques reported by Gulf News and analyzed by TechRadar move into standard toolkits for corporate security teams, that would signal that AI-based probing is becoming routine rather than experimental.

Finally, regulatory and standards bodies may begin to reference AI-assisted security testing more explicitly in their guidance. While the current reporting does not name specific forthcoming rules, DW’s broader coverage of AI and security suggests that policymakers are paying close attention. Any draft standards that mention AI models as both assets and potential attack tools would mark a further step into this new era of machine-speed cybersecurity.
