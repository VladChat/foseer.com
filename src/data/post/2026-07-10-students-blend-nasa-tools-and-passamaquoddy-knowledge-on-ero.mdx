For the Pleasant Point Passamaquoddy Reservation in Maine, known as Sipayik, the ocean has long been a teacher. Now, local students are using NASA science tools alongside Indigenous knowledge to study how that shoreline is changing.

According to a NASA feature on its science website, students are working with NASA-supported data and methods to examine coastal erosion around Sipayik and to connect those findings with what Passamaquoddy community members have observed over generations. The project is framed by NASA as a way to link satellite-era measurements with place-based understanding of the coast.

## What the students are doing

NASA’s account describes students engaging directly with coastal erosion, a process where waves, currents, and rising water levels wear away land along the shore. The students are using NASA science resources — such as Earth science data and analytical approaches — to document how the coastline near Sipayik is shifting over time.

Within this work, students are also drawing on Indigenous knowledge from the Passamaquoddy community. NASA’s description emphasizes that for Sipayik, the ocean has “always been a teacher,” and that the project encourages students to listen both to satellite-informed science and to stories, observations, and practices passed down in the community.

The NASA feature presents this as a hands-on learning effort: students are not only learning about erosion in the abstract but are examining changes along their own shoreline. The work centers specifically on the Pleasant Point Passamaquoddy Reservation and does not describe activities in other locations.

## How NASA science and Indigenous knowledge intersect

In its write-up, NASA characterizes the project as a connection between two ways of knowing the coast. On one side are NASA science tools, which can include satellite observations, maps, and data-driven methods for tracking land and water changes. On the other are long-term observations held within the Passamaquoddy community, including how the shoreline, ecosystems, and seasonal patterns have shifted over time.

NASA’s description indicates that students are encouraged to treat these sources as complementary rather than competing. For example, satellite-based measurements can show the rate and pattern of shoreline change over recent decades, while community knowledge can provide context about how those changes affect local fishing grounds, cultural sites, or everyday life.

The agency’s feature does not provide detailed technical specifications of the datasets or instruments involved, nor does it list specific classroom activities step by step. Instead, it focuses on the overall approach: using NASA-supported science education resources in a way that is grounded in the lived experience of Sipayik.

## Why this matters for Sipayik

NASA’s reporting highlights that the project is rooted in a place where the ocean is central to identity and daily life. For the Pleasant Point Passamaquoddy Reservation, coastal erosion is not only a scientific topic but a local reality that can affect land use, access to the water, and the stability of culturally important areas along the shore.

By involving students directly, the effort described by NASA gives young people in Sipayik tools to better understand what is happening to their coastline. It also positions them to ask informed questions about how the land and water around them are changing.

The NASA feature does not quantify the current rate of erosion at Sipayik or project future impacts. It also does not detail specific adaptation or protection measures. Instead, it focuses on the educational and knowledge-bridging aspects: students learning to read both satellite data and community experience as they examine the coast.

## What to watch next

Based on NASA’s account, this work is ongoing as an educational and community-engaged project. The agency’s article does not specify a fixed end date, nor does it list formal policy or engineering decisions that might follow from the students’ findings.

Key developments to watch, as more information becomes available, include:

- How students’ analyses of coastal change at Sipayik evolve as they work with additional data and community input
- Whether the approach of combining NASA science tools with Indigenous knowledge is extended to other classes or projects on the reservation
- Any future reporting from NASA or local partners that details specific findings about erosion patterns around the Pleasant Point Passamaquoddy Reservation

For now, NASA’s published account documents a focused effort: students at Sipayik using the agency’s science resources in tandem with Passamaquoddy knowledge to study a coastline that has shaped their community and continues to change before their eyes.
