Canadians who chose variable-rate mortgages as fixed borrowing costs climbed are now reassessing that decision after the latest move by the Bank of Canada. A recent analysis in The Globe and Mail asks whether variable rates are still worth considering, as the central bank’s policy path reshapes what homeowners pay on their loans.

The piece, published by The Globe and Mail and focused on Canadian borrowers, notes that many households had been opting for variable rates in recent months while bond market turmoil pushed fixed mortgage rates higher. The Bank of Canada’s latest decision has changed the calculation for some borrowers by affecting expectations for how interest costs could evolve over the life of a mortgage.

## What changed for variable-rate borrowers

The Globe and Mail reports that Canadians had been “flocking” to variable-rate mortgages over the past few months. That shift came as yields in the bond market rose, driving up the cost of fixed-rate mortgages that are typically tied to longer-term government bond yields.

Variable-rate mortgages in Canada are generally linked to a lender’s prime rate, which in turn is influenced by the Bank of Canada’s policy rate. When the central bank changes its key rate, the cost of variable borrowing usually moves in the same direction, often within days.

The article explains that the Bank of Canada’s latest decision has prompted lenders and borrowers to re-evaluate the relative appeal of variable versus fixed rates. While the specific rate move and its size are not detailed in the Globe and Mail piece summarized here, the analysis frames the decision as significant enough to alter the balance of risks between the two mortgage types.

## Why Canadians had been choosing variable rates

According to The Globe and Mail, the recent tilt toward variable mortgages followed a period of volatility in the bond market. That volatility pushed up yields on government bonds, a key benchmark used by lenders to price fixed-rate mortgages.

As fixed mortgage rates rose, some borrowers turned to variable loans, which initially offered lower interest costs. The Globe and Mail notes that this made variable rates attractive for households willing to accept the risk that their payments could increase if the Bank of Canada raised its policy rate again.

The article indicates that this was not a marginal shift: the phrase “flocking” suggests a noticeable change in borrower preferences, though the piece does not provide specific national figures or percentages. The core point, as reported, is that the relative pricing between fixed and variable products had tilted in favour of variable options before the Bank of Canada’s latest move.

## How the Bank of Canada’s decision affects the calculus

The Globe and Mail’s analysis focuses on a central question for borrowers: after the Bank of Canada’s latest decision, does it still make sense to take on the uncertainty of a variable rate?

Because variable mortgages are tied to the central bank’s policy rate through lenders’ prime rates, any change in that benchmark directly affects what borrowers pay. The article notes that the latest decision has immediate implications for those already in variable mortgages and for those considering whether to lock in a fixed rate instead.

The Globe and Mail frames the trade-off in practical terms. If the Bank of Canada is seen as being near the end of its tightening cycle, some borrowers might still find variable rates appealing, hoping that future rate cuts could lower their payments over time. If, by contrast, the central bank is expected to keep rates elevated or raise them further, the risk of higher future payments makes variable mortgages less attractive.

The article does not claim to predict the Bank of Canada’s next moves. Instead, it emphasizes that the latest decision has shifted expectations enough that borrowers should revisit earlier assumptions about how long higher rates might last.

## What is at stake for homeowners

For existing homeowners with variable-rate mortgages, The Globe and Mail notes that the Bank of Canada’s decision can affect monthly budgets quickly. When prime rates move, borrowers may see changes either in their monthly payment amount or in how much of each payment goes toward interest versus principal, depending on the structure of their mortgage.

The article underscores that this can be especially important for households that stretched to buy homes when rates were lower. For these borrowers, even a modest increase in borrowing costs can strain finances.

For prospective buyers, the question is whether to commit to a fixed rate now or accept the uncertainty of a variable loan in hopes that costs may ease later. The Globe and Mail’s reporting suggests that, after the Bank of Canada’s latest move, this decision has become more finely balanced than it appeared when fixed rates were rising sharply and variable options looked clearly cheaper at the outset.

## How to think about the choice now

The Globe and Mail’s piece does not offer a single recommendation for all borrowers. Instead, it presents the Bank of Canada’s latest decision as a prompt for Canadians to reassess their tolerance for risk and their financial flexibility.

The article indicates that variable mortgages may still appeal to borrowers who can handle potential payment increases and who believe that policy rates could eventually move lower. Fixed rates, by contrast, may be more suitable for those who prioritize certainty over the possibility of future savings.

The key message from the Globe and Mail analysis is that the environment that made variable rates look clearly advantageous—when bond-market-driven increases in fixed rates outpaced changes in variable pricing—has shifted with the Bank of Canada’s latest action. Borrowers who chose or considered variable mortgages during that earlier period are now being asked, by the central bank’s decision, to revisit whether that strategy still fits their circumstances.

## Why this matters now

The Globe and Mail’s reporting highlights that mortgage-rate decisions are not only about interest costs in isolation. They affect how much financial cushion households have, how resilient they are to further rate changes, and how they respond to evolving economic conditions.

With the Bank of Canada’s latest move altering the balance between fixed and variable options, Canadian borrowers face a more complex set of trade-offs than when fixed rates were clearly higher and variable loans offered immediate savings. The Globe and Mail’s central question—whether variable rates are still worth considering—captures a decision point that could shape household finances across the country in the months ahead.

Readers following this issue may wish to watch for further Bank of Canada decisions, as well as how lenders adjust their posted fixed and variable rates in response. Those changes will determine whether the current reassessment of variable mortgages becomes a short-lived pause or a more lasting shift back toward fixed-rate borrowing.
