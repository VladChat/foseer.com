Nvidia’s first earnings report since the ouster of long‑time chief executive Jensen Huang delivered a stark contrast: booming financial performance alongside a decisive move by the board to close the door on his return.

The company reported that quarterly revenue had doubled, according to coverage in *The Guardian*, underscoring the strength of demand for its products even amid leadership turmoil. During the same earnings call, Nvidia board chair Harvey Jones stated that the board "does not intend to reinstate Jensen as CEO," a remark reported by *The Guardian* and framed as a key moment in the company’s post‑Huang transition.

This combination of strong results and firm board messaging sets the stage for a critical period in which Nvidia must convince investors, customers, and regulators that it can maintain momentum without the executive who shaped its modern strategy.

## What Nvidia Reported and What the Board Said

In its latest quarterly earnings, Nvidia announced that revenue had doubled compared with the same period a year earlier, as reported by *The Guardian* [1]. While the detailed line‑item breakdown is not provided in the available coverage, both sources emphasize that this was the company’s first earnings release since Huang’s removal as CEO over allegations of an affair with a suspected Chinese spy [1][2].

On the earnings call, board chair Harvey Jones addressed the central question hanging over the company: whether Huang might be brought back. Jones said the board "does not intend to reinstate Jensen as CEO" [1]. That phrasing signals a clear direction from the board, even as it leaves room for standard corporate flexibility in legal terms.

CNBC’s coverage of the earnings, focused on questions for Nvidia’s leadership team, similarly centers Huang’s ouster and the board’s handling of the transition as the context for investor scrutiny [2]. Taken together, the two outlets present a consistent picture: Nvidia is financially strong in the short term, and the board is using that moment to underline that the leadership change is not temporary.

## Why the Board’s Stance Matters Despite Strong Numbers

From a purely financial perspective, a doubling of revenue typically strengthens a CEO’s position. The fact that Nvidia’s board is explicitly ruling out Huang’s return, even as it highlights strong results, is therefore significant.

Based on the reporting from *The Guardian* and CNBC, three points stand out:

1. **The board is separating performance from conduct.** The decision to remove Huang was tied to allegations about his personal conduct and a suspected Chinese spy, not to operational underperformance [1][2]. By reaffirming that he will not be reinstated, the board is emphasizing governance and risk considerations over the comfort of a familiar leader.

2. **The earnings call is being used to reset expectations.** Earnings calls are among the most closely watched corporate events. Jones’s comment during this call, rather than in a separate statement, suggests the board wanted investors and analysts to hear the message in the same breath as the strong revenue results [1].

3. **Short‑term strength does not resolve long‑term leadership questions.** Doubling revenue confirms that Nvidia’s existing product pipeline and market demand remain robust. But the board’s stance indicates it is planning for a future in which those assets are managed without Huang at the helm.

The evidence available does not specify how investors immediately reacted to the board’s statement. However, both outlets’ choice to highlight the board’s decision alongside the revenue surge points to its perceived materiality for Nvidia’s trajectory.

## Stakeholders: Who Gains, Who Faces New Risks

### Investors and Analysts

For shareholders, the earnings report delivers a mixed message. On one hand, the doubling of revenue suggests that Nvidia’s core business remains exceptionally strong [1]. On the other, the board’s clear refusal to consider Huang’s return introduces a structural change in how the company will be led.

CNBC’s framing of “key questions” around the earnings and leadership underscores that analysts are now evaluating Nvidia not only on its numbers but also on the credibility of its new governance setup [2]. Investors gain short‑term reassurance from the revenue figures, but they also face a new layer of uncertainty around execution without the architect of Nvidia’s long‑term vision.

### The Board and Current Leadership

The board’s position consolidates its authority. By publicly stating that Huang will not be reinstated, Jones and his fellow directors are signaling that they intend to see the transition through [1]. This can strengthen the hand of the current leadership team, which no longer has to operate under the shadow of a possible reversal.

At the same time, the board now owns the outcome. If Nvidia’s performance were to falter in future quarters, the decision not to bring back a historically successful CEO could become a focal point for criticism. The strong current revenue numbers give the board some political capital, but they also raise expectations that the company will maintain this level of performance.

### Customers and Partners

While the available reporting does not detail customer reactions, the basic dynamic is clear: customers care about product roadmaps, supply reliability, and long‑term support. A doubling of revenue signals that demand from these customers remains high [1].

The explicit confirmation that Huang will not return may be interpreted as a move toward stability—removing the overhang of speculation about leadership reversals. For large customers and partners, a clear governance line can be preferable to a prolonged period of uncertainty over who is really in charge.

## How the Two Sources Align—and What That Tells Us

The Guardian and CNBC, operating independently, converge on several core elements:

- Both identify this as Nvidia’s first earnings report since Huang’s ouster [1][2].
- Both emphasize the centrality of Huang’s removal and the board’s handling of it to how the earnings are being interpreted [1][2].
- Both repeatedly reference earnings, Nvidia, and Huang, indicating that the leadership question is not a side issue but part of the main story [1][2].

This alignment across two outlets and domains supports confidence in the basic facts: revenue has doubled, Huang has been ousted over misconduct‑related allegations, and the board has now publicly stated that it does not intend to bring him back as CEO.

Where the evidence is thinner is in the details: neither source, in the material available, provides a full quantitative breakdown of Nvidia’s results, a precise timeline for all internal board deliberations, or granular investor reaction data. That limits how far any analysis can go in attributing market behavior to the board’s decision versus the earnings themselves.

## How Likely Is Formal Confirmation in the Coming Week?

The reader’s specific question is how likely it is that Nvidia’s stance—doubling revenue while the board says it does not intend to reinstate Huang—will be “formally confirmed” in the next week.

On the revenue side, the doubling figure has already been reported by *The Guardian* based on Nvidia’s own quarterly announcement [1]. Corporate earnings releases are formal documents; the revenue result is therefore already formally confirmed by the company.

On the governance side, Harvey Jones’s statement that the board “does not intend to reinstate Jensen as CEO” was made on the earnings call and reported by *The Guardian* [1]. That is a public, on‑the‑record statement by the board chair. In that sense, the board’s position is already formally on record.

What could still change in the next week is the *form* of that confirmation. For example, Nvidia could issue additional written disclosures, or regulatory filings could reiterate the board’s stance. However, neither *The Guardian* nor CNBC reports any plan for such additional steps [1][2].

Based on the available evidence, the most reasonable assessment is:

- The core facts—revenue doubling and the board’s intent not to reinstate Huang—are already formally established by Nvidia’s earnings release and the chair’s public statement.
- Additional confirmation in the coming week is possible in routine follow‑up documents or interviews, but the key position does not depend on such follow‑ups to be considered “real.”

In other words, the probability that Nvidia will *reverse* this stance within a week appears low, given how definitively Jones spoke on the earnings call [1]. The probability that the company will merely restate the same position in another format is higher, but that would not materially change what is already known.

## What to Watch in the Weeks Ahead

Over the next several weeks to months, a few developments will shape how this moment is judged:

1. **Subsequent earnings performance.** If Nvidia sustains or builds on the doubled revenue reported in this quarter [1], the board’s decision to move on from Huang may be seen as compatible with continued operational strength. If performance weakens, pressure may grow to question the board’s judgment, even if a return by Huang is off the table.

2. **Board and leadership communication.** Further public comments, investor presentations, or filings could clarify how Nvidia’s leadership team plans to maintain momentum without Huang. While neither source reports a detailed roadmap yet [1][2], the tone and specificity of future communication will be key indicators of internal confidence.

3. **Market and analyst reactions.** CNBC’s focus on “key questions” for Nvidia suggests that analysts are actively probing the implications of Huang’s ouster [2]. Changes in analyst ratings, target prices, or commentary in the coming weeks will offer clues about whether the market views the board’s stance as a manageable governance issue or a deeper strategic risk.

Uncertainty remains, especially around how durable Nvidia’s current revenue surge will be and how effectively the new leadership structure will operate over time. But the central facts are now clear: Nvidia has delivered a strikingly strong quarter, and its board has chosen this moment to make an unambiguous statement that the Huang era is over.
