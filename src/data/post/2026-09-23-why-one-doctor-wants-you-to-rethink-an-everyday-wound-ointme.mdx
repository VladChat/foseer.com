Minor cuts and scrapes usually send people to the same spot in the drugstore aisle: the row of antibiotic ointments promising to prevent infection and speed healing. But at least one physician is urging patients to pause before reaching for those familiar tubes.

In a recent column for the Washington Post, a practicing doctor argued that routine use of over‑the‑counter topical antibiotic ointments for small, uncomplicated wounds is often unnecessary and can backfire, especially for people with sensitive skin. Instead, the doctor recommends a simpler approach centered on cleaning, protecting and letting the body’s own repair systems work.

While the column focuses on individual wound care, the message fits into a broader medical push to use antibiotics more selectively. The advice is aimed at everyday consumers who treat most scrapes at home, not at people with complex medical conditions or serious injuries.

## The Drugstore Habit This Doctor Wants to Change

The Washington Post column describes a familiar scene: someone nicks a finger in the kitchen or scrapes a knee on the sidewalk, rinses the area and then automatically reaches for a topical antibiotic ointment. These products typically combine multiple antibiotics in a petroleum‑based gel and are marketed for preventing infection in minor cuts, scrapes and burns.

According to the physician’s account, many patients assume these ointments are essential for proper healing. The doctor reports hearing versions of the same question in clinic: “What antibiotic cream should I put on it?” The column’s central argument is that, for most healthy people with small, clean wounds, the answer is often “none.”

The doctor notes that the skin already has robust defenses and that careful washing with soap and water, followed by a clean bandage, is usually enough to prevent infection in routine cuts and scrapes. The column stresses that this advice applies to minor injuries that are not heavily contaminated, deep, or located on high‑risk areas such as the face or hands where medical evaluation may be warranted.

## Why Many Minor Wounds Don’t Need Antibiotic Ointment

In the Washington Post piece, the physician explains that infection prevention in small wounds depends more on thorough cleaning than on applying antibiotic creams. The column emphasizes several points:

- **Mechanical cleaning is key.** Gently washing the wound with mild soap and running water removes dirt and bacteria from the surface. The doctor describes this step as the most important part of home wound care.

- **A moist, protected environment helps healing.** Covering the wound with a clean bandage or dressing keeps it from drying out and protects it from further irritation. The column notes that this kind of basic protection is often enough for healthy skin to repair itself.

- **The body’s immune system does most of the work.** For people without serious underlying conditions, the immune system typically handles the small number of bacteria that may remain after cleaning a minor cut.

The physician acknowledges that topical antibiotics can be appropriate in some situations, particularly when a clinician recommends them for higher‑risk wounds. But the column argues that automatic, routine use for every scrape is not supported by the way most minor injuries actually heal.

## Risks the Doctor Sees in Routine Ointment Use

The Washington Post column also details why the physician has grown cautious about recommending these products for everyday scrapes. The concerns focus on side effects and the potential for allergic reactions.

The doctor describes seeing patients develop red, itchy, or blistering rashes where an antibiotic ointment had been applied. In some cases, the reaction can resemble or even be mistaken for an infection, leading to more worry and additional treatment.

According to the column, these reactions are a form of contact dermatitis — skin inflammation triggered by direct contact with a substance. The physician notes that some of the antibiotic ingredients commonly found in over‑the‑counter ointments are known to cause allergic reactions in a subset of people.

The column also points out a practical downside: when a wound becomes more irritated after ointment use, it can be harder for both patients and clinicians to tell what is going on. Is the redness a normal part of healing, a sign of infection, or a reaction to the product itself? That uncertainty can lead to extra visits, additional medications, and more anxiety for patients.

## What This Doctor Recommends Instead

In place of automatic antibiotic ointment use, the physician lays out a straightforward home‑care routine for minor cuts and scrapes in the Washington Post column:

1. **Rinse and clean.** As soon as possible after the injury, hold the area under running water and gently wash with mild soap to remove visible dirt and debris.
2. **Stop the bleeding.** Apply gentle pressure with a clean cloth or bandage until bleeding slows or stops.
3. **Protect the wound.** Cover the area with a clean adhesive bandage or sterile dressing. The doctor suggests changing the bandage daily or sooner if it becomes wet or dirty.
4. **Watch for warning signs.** The column advises patients to monitor for increasing redness, warmth, swelling, pain, or pus — changes that can indicate infection and should prompt medical evaluation.

When patients ask what to put on the wound, the physician often recommends simple, non‑medicated options instead of antibiotic creams, according to the column. The focus is on keeping the area clean and lightly moist, rather than on adding multiple antibiotic ingredients by default.

The doctor also underscores that some wounds do need professional care. Deep cuts, bites, puncture wounds, injuries on the face or hands, and wounds in people with diabetes or other conditions that affect healing are examples where the column advises seeking medical attention rather than relying on home care alone.

## Why This Advice Matters for Everyday Patients

The doctor’s message, as presented in the Washington Post, is aimed at changing a small but common habit: reaching for an antibiotic ointment every time skin is broken. The column suggests that rethinking this reflex could spare some people from avoidable skin reactions and confusion about whether a wound is infected.

For readers, the takeaway is narrow but practical. For minor, clean cuts and scrapes in otherwise healthy people, the physician’s account suggests that careful washing, simple bandaging and watchful waiting are often enough — and that more medication is not always better.

The column does not call for policy changes or new regulations. Instead, it offers a shift in everyday practice, grounded in one doctor’s clinical experience and understanding of how uncomplicated wounds typically heal. As more patients encounter this advice in clinic visits and media coverage, the question of when to use topical antibiotics — and when to skip them — is likely to remain a point of discussion between patients and their clinicians.
