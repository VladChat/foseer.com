Federal regulators have cited Island Kinetics, Inc., which does business as CoValence Laboratories, for a series of violations involving its over‑the‑counter (OTC) drug products, including failures to meet current good manufacturing practice (CGMP) standards and concerns over unapproved new drugs, misbranding, and adulteration.

According to a public notice from the U.S. Food and Drug Administration (FDA), the agency’s findings center on how CoValence manufactures and labels certain finished pharmaceutical products that are marketed as OTC drugs. The FDA notice is the primary source of information currently available about the case.

## What the FDA says CoValence did wrong

In its notice, the FDA states that Island Kinetics, Inc. d.b.a. CoValence Laboratories is associated with multiple categories of regulatory concern:

- **CGMP** (current good manufacturing practice) deficiencies
- **Finished pharmaceuticals** issues
- **Unapproved new drug** violations
- **Misbranding** violations
- **Adulteration** of OTC drug products

CGMP refers to the minimum standards that manufacturers must meet to ensure their products are consistently produced and controlled for quality. When the FDA characterizes products as adulterated in this context, it typically means they were prepared, packed, or held under conditions that do not comply with CGMP requirements.

The FDA’s notice groups CoValence under firms handling finished pharmaceuticals, indicating that the agency’s concerns relate to the manufacturing and distribution of products that are already in final dosage form and ready for consumer use.

The agency also identifies CoValence in connection with unapproved new drugs and misbranding. Under U.S. law, an unapproved new drug is a product that meets the definition of a drug and is intended for therapeutic use but has not received FDA approval through the new drug application process. Misbranding generally refers to problems with a product’s labeling or promotional claims, such as labels that are false or misleading, or that fail to include required information like adequate directions for use.

The FDA notice links these issues specifically to OTC products, meaning drugs that are sold directly to consumers without a prescription. The document does not, however, provide a public list of individual product names in the portion currently available.

## How the FDA framed the violations

The FDA’s public communication places CoValence within a broader enforcement category that combines several types of violations: CGMP failures, unapproved new drug marketing, misbranding, and adulteration of OTC finished pharmaceuticals. While the notice does not spell out each specific observation from inspections, its categorization signals that regulators see overlapping problems in both the quality systems used to make the products and the way some of those products are positioned or labeled in the market.

By highlighting CGMP and adulteration together, the agency is indicating that it considers the manufacturing controls at CoValence to be inadequate under federal standards. When FDA uses the term adulterated in this setting, it is not necessarily alleging contamination of a specific batch, but rather that the firm’s methods, facilities, or controls do not meet the legal requirements designed to assure product quality.

The pairing of unapproved new drug and misbranding concerns suggests that, in FDA’s view, some CoValence products may be making therapeutic claims or presenting themselves as drugs in ways that require formal approval or more stringent labeling than they currently have. However, the public notice does not detail which claims or labels triggered that assessment.

## What is known — and what is not

All currently available details about the FDA’s findings against Island Kinetics, Inc. d.b.a. CoValence Laboratories come from an FDA.gov posting that lists the company in connection with CGMP, finished pharmaceuticals, unapproved new drugs, misbranding, adulteration, and OTC products.

The agency’s notice does not publicly specify:

- The exact dates of the inspection or inspections
- The individual OTC products involved
- The precise language on labels or in marketing materials that the FDA considers problematic
- Whether any specific product lots have been recalled

Independent corroboration of the details beyond the FDA posting is limited at this time. No additional public regulatory filings, court records, or company statements were identified in the same evidence set. As a result, the FDA notice remains the central, and currently only, authoritative description of the case available for this report.

The FDA’s public listing also does not include a company response. It is common in such cases for firms to receive a formal communication from the agency and then submit written plans to correct the cited issues, but the existence or content of any such response from CoValence is not described in the material reviewed.

## Why this matters for patients and providers

The FDA’s action matters because it touches on both **how** OTC drugs are made and **how** they are presented to the public.

First, CGMP and adulteration findings raise questions about the reliability and consistency of the affected products. Even when no specific injury reports are cited, FDA uses CGMP rules to prevent problems before they reach consumers. When a manufacturer of finished pharmaceuticals does not meet those standards, regulators view it as a risk to the quality of medicines people may already be using.

Second, the unapproved new drug and misbranding designations speak to how patients and health professionals understand what a product can and cannot do. If an OTC item is sold with drug‑like claims but has not gone through FDA’s approval process, or if its labeling omits required information, consumers and clinicians may not have the basis they need to judge its safety and effectiveness.

In this case, the FDA has not publicly reported specific adverse events tied to CoValence products in the material reviewed. The agency’s notice instead focuses on regulatory and manufacturing compliance. For patients and providers, that means the concern is about systemic safeguards rather than a documented pattern of harm — at least based on what is currently disclosed.

## What to watch next

Because the FDA’s public notice is the only detailed source currently available, the next developments to watch will likely come from additional regulatory documents or any future communication from Island Kinetics, Inc. d.b.a. CoValence Laboratories.

Key questions that further reporting could clarify include:

- Whether the company initiates voluntary recalls or product changes
- How CoValence addresses the FDA’s CGMP concerns in its manufacturing operations
- Whether the FDA releases more detailed inspection findings or follow‑up actions

For now, the FDA has formally placed CoValence in a category that signals significant concerns about CGMP compliance, product status as unapproved new drugs, misbranding, and adulteration of OTC finished pharmaceuticals. Until more information emerges, that federal assessment is the main public record of what regulators believe has gone wrong at the company — and why they are pressing for change.
