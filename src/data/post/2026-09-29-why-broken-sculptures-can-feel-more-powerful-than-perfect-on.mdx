Why do some of the most memorable sculptures in museums lack noses, arms or even whole faces—and yet stay with us long after we leave the gallery?

A new exhibition reported by *the Guardian* explores this puzzle through fragments: pouting lips from an Egyptian pharaoh, the shattered head of Benito Mussolini, and other damaged works that seem to gain power from what they have lost. The show asks visitors to look again at what is missing, and what that absence makes us feel.

This explainer walks through what the exhibition highlights, why broken statues can feel so intense, and how damage changes the way we see power, beauty and history.

## What this exhibition is actually showing

The exhibition, described in detail by *the Guardian*, is built around a simple but striking idea: instead of presenting only complete statues and monuments, it foregrounds fragments—pieces that time, violence or accident have left incomplete.

Among the works the paper highlights are:

- **A pharaoh’s lips** – described as “stern, sensuous, almost kissable.” The rest of the face is gone, but the mouth remains, isolated and strangely intimate.
- **A damaged head of Benito Mussolini** – the Italian dictator’s image survives as a broken sculpture with a hole in the head, the wound turning a symbol of authority into something vulnerable and unsettling.

According to *the Guardian*, the exhibition builds its argument through juxtapositions like these: rulers and gods shown not as flawless marble ideals but as partial, injured, or eroded forms. Rather than hiding the damage, the curators put it front and center.

The show also echoes a famous line by the 17th‑century French philosopher Blaise Pascal, who wrote that if Cleopatra’s nose had been shorter, “the whole face of the earth would have changed.” The exhibition uses this idea—that a single facial feature can reshape history—as a kind of thought experiment about how we project meaning onto faces, and what happens when those faces are broken.

## Why broken statues can feel more powerful than perfect ones

The central claim, as reported by *the Guardian*, is that some sculptures grow more powerful when they are incomplete. That idea runs against the way many of us were taught to think about art, where damage is usually seen as loss.

The exhibition suggests several ways brokenness can add force:

1. **Focus through subtraction**  
   When only part of a figure survives—like the pharaoh’s lips—our attention narrows. Instead of scanning a whole body or face, we are drawn into one charged detail: a mouth that looks stern yet inviting, or an eye that seems to stare out more intensely because the rest of the head is missing. The missing parts make what remains feel more concentrated.

2. **Space for imagination**  
   A complete statue tells us exactly what the artist wanted us to see. A fragment leaves gaps that viewers fill in themselves. According to the exhibition’s framing, this can make the experience more active: we imagine the full figure, the original setting, and the events that led to its damage. The work becomes a prompt for storytelling rather than a finished statement.

3. **Visible history of violence and time**  
   Cracks, missing noses and shattered skulls are not just defects; they are physical records of what has happened to an object over centuries. In the Mussolini head with a hole in it, the damage reads like a counter‑argument to the dictator’s self‑image. Where a polished, intact bust would project authority, the broken version exposes how power can be challenged, toppled or literally struck down.

In all these cases, the force of the work comes not despite the damage but through it. The exhibition, as described by *the Guardian*, invites viewers to see each break as part of the sculpture’s meaning.

## How damage changes our sense of power and beauty

The show also asks a more uncomfortable question: what does it mean when the images that survive of powerful people are damaged ones?

Using Mussolini’s broken head as a focal point, the exhibition highlights how shattered monuments can become tools for rethinking authority. A dictator’s portrait, meant to inspire fear or obedience, becomes a reminder of his downfall when it is chipped, pierced or toppled.

With the pharaoh’s lips, the effect is different but related. The fragment still suggests royal control—the lips are “stern”—but they are also described as “sensuous, almost kissable.” That tension between command and intimacy is heightened by the fact that we see only the mouth. The absence of the rest of the face lets the viewer project feelings and interpretations that might be less intense if the statue were intact.

The Cleopatra reference, drawn from Pascal and invoked in the exhibition coverage, underlines how much we read into features like noses, mouths and eyes. Cleopatra’s supposed beauty has long been used to explain political events in the ancient Mediterranean. By focusing on broken faces and missing features, the exhibition pushes visitors to notice how fragile those narratives are—and how easily they can be reshaped when the image itself is damaged.

## Why museums are rethinking fragments

Fragments have always been part of museum collections, but they have not always been treated as central objects. Often, broken pieces were displayed as supporting material, or even stored out of sight.

The exhibition described by *the Guardian* reflects a shift in how institutions present these works. Instead of treating damage as something to be corrected or hidden, curators are increasingly treating it as a key to understanding the object’s life.

In practical terms, that means:

- **Placing fragments in prime positions**, rather than in side cases.
- **Writing labels and wall texts that explain the breakage**, including whether it came from deliberate iconoclasm (the targeted destruction of images) or from gradual wear.
- **Encouraging visitors to think about absence**, not just presence—what was removed, by whom, and why.

The exhibition uses the pharaoh and Mussolini examples to make that case in concrete, visual terms. Visitors do not just read about the idea that damage can be meaningful; they see it etched into stone and carved into faces.

## What this means for how we look at sculpture

The show’s argument, as relayed by *the Guardian*, is less about rewriting art history than about changing how we look.

Instead of asking “What did this statue look like when it was new?”, the exhibition nudges visitors to ask:

- What has this object been through?  
- How does the damage change what it says about beauty or power?  
- What emotions do the surviving fragments provoke that a perfect statue might not?

The pharaoh’s lips and Mussolini’s broken head serve as clear examples. One turns royal authority into something unexpectedly intimate. The other turns a self‑image of invincibility into a reminder of mortality and defeat.

By the time visitors leave, the goal is that they will see broken sculptures across other collections differently—not as failed or incomplete versions of something better, but as objects whose power is inseparable from their scars.

## Key takeaways

From the reporting on this exhibition, several points stand out:

- Fragments like a pharaoh’s lips or a dictator’s damaged head can feel more intense than complete statues because they focus attention and invite imagination.
- Damage is not just loss; it records time, conflict and changing attitudes toward power and beauty.
- References such as Pascal’s line about Cleopatra’s nose highlight how much we project onto faces—and how broken faces can unsettle those stories.
- Museums are increasingly treating broken works as central, not secondary, using them to help visitors think about history as something lived and contested, not frozen in marble.

For readers and museum‑goers, the exhibition’s message is straightforward: the next time you see a statue without a nose or a bust with a cracked skull, it may be worth pausing. The missing pieces might be where the story really begins.
