Most Americans tell pollsters they are worried about the climate crisis and want political leaders to act. Yet during recent US midterm campaigns, many candidates in tightly contested races barely mentioned climate change at all.

This gap between voter concern and campaign silence has been described by some analysts as “climate hushing” — the choice to downplay or sidestep climate in public messaging even when candidates have climate plans on paper.

Based on reporting from the *Guardian* on battleground races, this explainer walks through what is known about voter attitudes, how candidates are talking (or not talking) about climate, and why that disconnect matters for policy.

## What voters say they want on climate

Polling cited by the *Guardian* indicates that concern about climate change is not limited to one party or region.

Surveys in battleground states — the competitive states that often decide control of Congress — have found:

- **Most respondents report unease or alarm about the climate crisis.** The *Guardian* describes a majority of Americans as feeling either uneasy or alarmed about global heating.
- **Voters in both Democratic-leaning and Republican-leaning states want to hear more about climate.** According to the reporting, this pattern appears in red and blue states alike, suggesting the interest is broader than just the Democratic base.
- **People want to know what leaders will *do*, not just what they believe.** The *Guardian* notes that voters are looking for concrete explanations of how political leaders plan to fix or address the problem.

These findings do not mean every voter ranks climate as their single top issue, but they do show a consistent desire for information and action, including among voters who may disagree on other policies.

## What “climate hushing” looks like in campaigns

Despite that interest, the *Guardian* reports that many midterm candidates have responded with “near silence” on the climate crisis.

In practice, that silence can take several forms:

- **Omitting climate from stump speeches and ads.** Candidates may focus on inflation, crime, or other topics while rarely mentioning climate, even if their websites contain climate policy sections.
- **Using vague language instead of explicit climate framing.** Some campaigns talk about “clean energy jobs” or “resilience” without directly linking those ideas to global heating.
- **Limiting climate talk to friendly audiences.** The article describes a pattern where climate is more likely to come up in targeted settings — such as events with environmental groups — but not in broad, high-visibility messaging.

The *Guardian*’s reporting emphasizes that this pattern is especially visible in **battleground races**, where candidates are highly sensitive to perceived political risk and where small shifts in voter turnout can decide outcomes.

## Why campaigns might stay quiet despite voter concern

The *Guardian* article does not present a single definitive explanation, but it points to several factors that, together, may help explain the “climate hushing” pattern.

### 1. Campaigns fear climate talk could be politically risky

According to strategists and advocates interviewed by the *Guardian*, many campaign teams still see climate as a potentially polarizing topic, even when polls show broad concern.

Several dynamics feed that perception:

- **Partisan stereotypes.** Climate has long been framed in US politics as a “Democratic” issue. Some candidates in competitive districts may worry that strong climate messaging will be portrayed as extreme or anti-business.
- **Attack-ad anxiety.** Campaigns fear that opponents will use any mention of climate to tie them to unpopular ideas, such as higher gasoline prices or restrictions on driving, even when those positions are not in their plans.

The reporting suggests that, as a result, some candidates choose to minimize explicit climate talk rather than risk being defined by it.

### 2. Consultants prioritize short-term, poll-tested messages

The *Guardian* describes how professional campaign consultants often guide candidates toward issues that show immediate, measurable returns in polling and focus groups.

In this environment:

- **Economic and cost-of-living messages dominate.** Consultants may see inflation, wages, and healthcare costs as safer, more urgent topics to emphasize.
- **Climate is treated as secondary, even when voters care.** If climate does not appear as the top issue in polling, it can be pushed down the message hierarchy, despite underlying concern.

This does not mean campaigns ignore climate entirely, but it can mean that climate rarely leads their messaging.

### 3. Climate impacts are widespread but politically diffuse

The *Guardian* notes that many Americans are already experiencing climate-linked events — such as extreme heat or more intense storms — but these experiences do not always translate into clear political demands.

Several features make climate harder to campaign on:

- **Complex causes and timelines.** Voters may connect a heatwave or wildfire to climate change in general, but it is harder to link that directly to a specific candidate’s policy.
- **Shared responsibility.** Because climate is a global problem built up over decades, no single party or politician can credibly claim to have “solved” it, which can make it a less attractive campaign message.

The article suggests that this complexity can make climate feel less immediately “usable” in campaign advertising, even as underlying concern grows.

## How TikTok and social media fit into the picture

The story also touches on the role of platforms like TikTok in shaping how climate appears in political conversation.

According to the *Guardian*’s reporting:

- **Climate content is highly visible on TikTok and other platforms.** Young creators and activists regularly post about climate anxiety, extreme weather, and policy debates.
- **This online energy does not always translate into candidate messaging.** While climate-related videos may go viral, many official campaign accounts still devote more space to traditional talking points.

The article indicates that this creates a kind of split-screen effect: climate is prominent in grassroots and youth-driven online spaces, but still relatively muted in formal campaign rhetoric.

## Why this silence matters for policy

The gap between voter concern and campaign messaging has practical consequences, as described in the *Guardian* report.

- **Less public debate on concrete solutions.** When candidates avoid detailed climate talk, voters hear less about specific plans for cutting emissions, preparing for extreme weather, or investing in clean energy.
- **Limited mandate after elections.** If climate is not a visible campaign issue, elected officials may feel they lack a clear public mandate to pursue ambitious climate policy, even if voters are worried.
- **Missed chance to connect climate to daily life.** The article notes that many Americans already feel the effects of global heating. When campaigns stay quiet, they miss opportunities to explain how policies could reduce risks or costs for ordinary people.

In short, “climate hushing” is not just about rhetoric. It shapes which problems are treated as urgent in governing, and which remain in the background.

## What readers should watch in future campaigns

The *Guardian*’s reporting on recent midterms suggests a tension that is unlikely to disappear quickly: voters across party lines express unease or alarm about the climate crisis, but many candidates still treat climate as a secondary or risky topic.

Key questions going forward include:

- Whether campaigns in competitive states begin to talk more directly about climate as polling continues to show concern.
- How younger voters — who are highly active on platforms like TikTok — respond when they see climate discussed more online than in official campaign messaging.

For now, the evidence presented in the *Guardian* points to a clear pattern: public anxiety about global heating is real and measurable, but in many high-stakes races, climate remains largely offstage.
