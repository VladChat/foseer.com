For much of the last decade, the United States men’s national team has been framed around one central figure: Christian Pulisic. He has been the face of the program, its leading attacker, and, in the American soccer imagination, the player meant to drag the team forward almost single‑handedly.

So when the USMNT recently produced strong performances and results without Pulisic on the field, it raised a pointed question, captured by the Guardian’s reporting: with no Pulisic and good results, has the team finally moved on from its savior complex?

Those matches did not settle the debate, but they did offer a glimpse of a squad that looks less like a one‑man project and more like a functioning collective.

## The end of the one‑man script?

The Guardian’s account of the team’s recent run notes a key contrast: in games where Pulisic was unavailable, the US still found ways to control play, create chances and win. That matters because, for years, the USMNT’s attacking plan often seemed to default to “get the ball to Pulisic and hope.”

In these latest outings, other players stepped into more prominent roles. The Guardian describes a side that shared responsibility across the front line and midfield, rather than waiting for one star to solve every problem. That shift, even over a small sample of matches, is significant in a program long conditioned to look for a singular hero.

The results themselves, as reported, were not flukes scraped out against overwhelming pressure. Instead, they reflected a team capable of coherent buildup and coordinated pressing without its marquee attacker. That is a different kind of proof of concept: not that the US can survive Pulisic’s absence, but that it can function with a different attacking center of gravity.

## How the savior complex took hold

The Guardian’s framing of a “savior complex” did not appear in a vacuum. It reflects how Pulisic’s rise has often been narrated in US soccer culture. From his early breakthrough in Europe to his starring role in World Cup qualifying, Pulisic was cast as the long‑awaited answer to the question of whether the US could produce a world‑class attacker.

That storyline hardened after painful moments, including the failure to reach the 2018 World Cup. In the public conversation, Pulisic was frequently portrayed as the player who would ensure such a collapse never happened again. The Guardian’s piece taps into that history: a team’s fortunes tied emotionally, and sometimes tactically, to one man’s form and fitness.

Within that context, every match without Pulisic has carried a second layer of meaning. It is not just about the scoreline. It is about whether the team can look like itself without its presumed centerpiece. The Guardian’s reporting suggests that, in this latest stretch, the answer was more convincingly “yes” than in previous cycles.

## What changes when the attack is shared

In describing the team’s recent play, the Guardian highlights an important qualitative shift: multiple players taking on creative and goal‑scoring responsibility, rather than deferring to Pulisic’s instincts and reputation.

That redistribution shows up in several ways:

- **More varied buildup**: Instead of funneling possession to one flank or one player, the US used different routes to progress the ball. The Guardian’s account points to a side that could combine through midfield and switch play, making it harder for opponents to key on a single threat.

- **Rotating focal points**: With Pulisic absent, others were forced to receive under pressure, attack defenders and make final‑third decisions. The Guardian notes that this did not lead to chaos or hesitation; it led to a more even spread of responsibility.

- **Psychological effect**: The very fact that the US produced good results without their star undermines the idea that everything collapses when he is missing. The Guardian’s framing of the question — whether the savior complex is fading — reflects how those performances may shift expectations inside and outside the squad.

None of this means Pulisic has become less important. The Guardian does not suggest that. Instead, the reporting points to a team that might be learning how to make better use of him: as a leading figure in a larger cast, rather than the only actor on stage.

## Stakes for the players and the program

The Guardian’s focus on the savior narrative highlights what is at stake for different groups around the team.

For Pulisic himself, the burden of being treated as a singular solution has always been double‑edged. On one hand, it is a mark of his talent and status within the squad. On the other, it loads every injury, dip in form, or quiet game with outsized meaning. The Guardian’s question hints at a scenario where he can still be the team’s star, but not its sole hope.

For his teammates, the recent results create room to be seen — and to see themselves — as more than supporting characters. The Guardian’s reporting on the collective performances without Pulisic underscores how others used that space to assert their own influence on matches.

For the broader US program, including its relationship with FIFA competitions, the implications are more subtle but still real. A national team less dependent on one player is better equipped to handle the disruptions that inevitably come with international football: injuries, club‑country conflicts, and congested schedules. The Guardian’s account of the team’s resilience without Pulisic suggests a step in that direction, even if it is only an early one.

## Has the savior complex really faded?

The Guardian poses its central question — whether the USMNT has moved on from its savior complex — without declaring the issue settled. The evidence from this particular run of matches is encouraging for those who want to see a more balanced team, but it is also limited.

A few points emerge from the reporting:

- **Short‑term proof, long‑term question**: The Guardian’s description of good results without Pulisic shows that the US can win and play coherent football in his absence. Whether that becomes the norm, rather than an exception, remains to be seen.

- **Narratives move slower than tactics**: Even if the team’s on‑field structure is less Pulisic‑centric, public and media narratives often lag behind. The Guardian’s decision to frame the discussion around the savior complex indicates that this storyline still has a strong hold on how the team is perceived.

- **Independent corroboration is still thin**: As the Guardian itself notes, independent corroboration of broader conclusions in this cycle is limited and should be monitored as more reporting and more matches arrive. That caution matters. One outlet’s framing, however influential, does not by itself prove a lasting cultural shift.

In other words, the recent games show what a less star‑dependent USMNT can look like. They do not yet prove that the program — or its surrounding ecosystem — has fully embraced that identity.

## What to watch as the cycle unfolds

The Guardian’s reporting sets up a series of practical questions that will shape how this story develops:

- **How is Pulisic used when he returns?** If the team continues to share attacking responsibility even with him on the pitch, that would be a stronger sign that the savior model is fading.

- **Do others keep stepping up in big moments?** The Guardian’s emphasis on recent results without Pulisic points to a crucial test: whether those same players can deliver when the stakes rise in FIFA competitions.

- **Does the public narrative adjust?** The persistence or decline of the savior complex will not only be about tactics. It will also be about whether fans and media, including major outlets like the Guardian, continue to treat every USMNT storyline as a referendum on one player.

For now, the clearest thing the Guardian’s coverage establishes is that the USMNT no longer looks helpless without Christian Pulisic. That alone marks a meaningful shift from earlier cycles. Whether it becomes the foundation of a new identity — one in which a star can be central without being singular — will be written in the matches still to come.
