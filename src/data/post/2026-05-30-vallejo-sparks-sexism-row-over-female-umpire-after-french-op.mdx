Adolfo Daniel Vallejo has triggered a sexism row at the French Open after suggesting his second‑round match should not have been overseen by a woman, arguing that Brazilian chair umpire Ana Carvalho was unable to control an intense home crowd.

The Paraguayan player made the remarks after losing to French teenager Moïse Kouamé, telling reporters that “this sort of match needs a man” in the chair and blaming Carvalho for allowing spectators to become, in his words, “very out of line”, according to reporting by *the Guardian*.

## A heated match and a partisan crowd

Vallejo’s comments came in the immediate aftermath of his defeat to Kouamé in Paris, during a match played in front of a strongly pro‑French crowd. The Guardian’s account describes the atmosphere as highly charged, with home supporters vocally backing the teenage French player.

Following the loss, Vallejo focused his criticism on Carvalho rather than on his own performance or the crowd itself. He argued that the Brazilian umpire failed to keep the stands under control and linked that perceived failure directly to her gender, saying the match required a male official.

While crowd noise and partisan support are common features of Grand Slam tennis, Vallejo framed the situation as one in which the chair umpire should have intervened more forcefully. The Guardian report notes that, in his view, Carvalho did not do enough to address what he described as inappropriate behavior from spectators.

## Targeting the umpire’s gender

What has drawn the most attention is not that Vallejo disputed the handling of the crowd, but that he connected his criticism explicitly to Carvalho’s being a woman.

According to the Guardian report, Vallejo argued that a male umpire would have been better suited to manage the match, saying that “this sort of match needs a man”. By tying crowd control to gender rather than experience, authority, or specific decisions, his remarks have been widely interpreted as sexist.

The Guardian account does not indicate that Vallejo provided evidence for why a male umpire would have been more effective, beyond his personal belief. Nor does it report any suggestion from him that Carvalho had breached rules or failed to apply specific procedures; his criticism, as described, centers on her perceived inability to rein in the crowd and his assumption that a man would have done so.

## Reaction and emerging controversy

Vallejo’s comments, as reported, have sparked controversy because they appear to question the suitability of women to officiate high‑pressure matches. The Guardian coverage highlights that his remarks have been received as an attack not only on one umpire’s performance but on women’s place in top‑level officiating.

The report does not yet detail official responses from tournament organizers, tennis governing bodies, or player organizations. It also does not record any public statement from Carvalho herself. As a result, the current picture of the controversy is based primarily on Vallejo’s post‑match remarks and how they have been relayed and framed in early reporting.

What is clear from the Guardian’s account is that his words have shifted attention away from the sporting result and toward questions of respect for officials and the treatment of women in professional tennis roles.

## What is at stake

Vallejo’s comments go beyond a routine dispute over officiating. By attributing the handling of a difficult match environment to the umpire’s gender, he has raised concerns about how female officials are perceived and treated in elite tennis.

At issue is whether players accept women as fully authoritative in the chair, particularly in emotionally charged matches involving partisan crowds. The Guardian’s reporting shows that, in this instance, a player publicly questioned that authority on explicitly gendered grounds.

For Carvalho, the incident centers on her professional reputation, with criticism aimed not only at specific decisions but at her capacity to control a match. For Vallejo, the fallout may shape how he is viewed within the sport, especially by colleagues and officials who work alongside female umpires.

While further responses from tennis authorities and other players have not yet been detailed in available reporting, the episode has already become a flashpoint in the ongoing conversation about respect for officials and the role of women in high‑visibility positions in sport.

## Why this matters now

The Guardian’s account of Vallejo’s remarks underscores how quickly a single post‑match interview can ignite a wider debate. A routine second‑round match at a Grand Slam has turned into a test of how the sport responds when a player appears to undermine a female official’s authority on the basis of gender.

As more reactions emerge, key questions will include whether tournament organizers address the comments directly, whether Vallejo clarifies or revises his remarks, and how fellow players and officials respond to an incident that touches on professionalism, respect, and gender equality in tennis.
