Dwayne Johnson’s hair — or more precisely, his new hairpiece for the upcoming live‑action version of *Moana* — has become an online talking point. A single styling choice for a family film has turned into a wider debate about how beloved animated characters are translated to live action.

This explainer walks through what we actually know, why the wig is attracting attention, and how to make sense of the reactions without getting lost in social‑media noise.

## What happened with Dwayne Johnson’s ‘Moana’ hairpiece?

According to a report in *The Guardian* about early reactions to the live‑action *Moana*, viewers have zeroed in on one detail: the new hairpiece worn by Dwayne Johnson in his role as Maui. The article describes how the wig — sometimes referred to in headlines as Johnson’s new “barnet,” British slang for haircut or hairstyle — has become a focal point of discussion around the film.

In the original 2016 animated *Moana*, Maui is a demigod with a very distinctive mane of long, curly hair. In the live‑action version now in development, Johnson is using a wig to recreate that look. The Guardian report notes that images and footage showing this new hairpiece have circulated widely, prompting a wave of comments, jokes and debate about how well the styling works.

The basic facts, as reported:

- Johnson is returning as Maui in a live‑action remake of *Moana*.
- To play the character, he is wearing a wig designed to echo Maui’s animated hair.
- That wig has become a talking point online and in entertainment coverage, including the Guardian piece asking why everyone is “wigging out” over it.

## Why are people reacting so strongly to a wig?

The Guardian’s coverage makes clear that the hairpiece is not just a trivial detail for many viewers. Instead, it has become a shorthand for broader questions about how faithfully the live‑action film will capture the spirit and look of the original.

Several overlapping reactions emerge from that reporting:

- **Fidelity to the animated design:** Maui’s hair is a key part of his animated silhouette. When a live‑action version changes or exaggerates that look, fans notice quickly.
- **Expectations around realism:** A wig that looks too polished, too stiff or too theatrical can feel out of place in a live‑action setting, even if it matches the cartoon closely.
- **The visibility of the change:** Because Johnson is widely known for his bald or closely shaved look, any dramatic hairstyle on him stands out more than it might on another actor.

The Guardian article frames the reaction as part amusement, part concern. Some viewers treat the wig as a light‑hearted meme; others see it as an early warning sign about the film’s creative choices. The intensity of the response, in other words, is less about hair itself and more about what the hair is taken to represent.

## How does Maui look in the original ‘Moana’?

To understand why the wig matters to audiences, it helps to recall what Maui looks like in the animated film. As described in the Guardian’s piece, Maui is presented there as a physically imposing demigod with long, thick, curly hair that moves and bounces with him.

That animated hair serves several functions:

- **Character identity:** Maui’s mane is part of his larger‑than‑life persona. It helps distinguish him visually from other characters.
- **Cultural styling:** The hair, along with Maui’s tattoos and clothing, contributes to a specific visual language inspired by Pacific Islander cultures.
- **Animation tool:** In animation, hair can exaggerate motion and emotion. When Maui laughs, fights or sings, his hair helps sell the action.

The live‑action wig is trying to echo this design. According to the Guardian coverage, the question being asked by commentators is whether the new hairpiece successfully captures that energy or ends up feeling like a costume sitting on top of an otherwise grounded performance.

## What exactly is being criticized — the wig or the adaptation?

Based on the Guardian’s reporting, the hairpiece is acting as a stand‑in for broader anxieties about the live‑action remake.

The criticisms fall into two main buckets:

- **Aesthetic concerns:** Some viewers think the wig looks too artificial or stylized when seen in photos or clips. They worry that it could distract from the story if it draws too much attention to itself.
- **Adaptation worries:** Others use the wig as a symbol of their doubts about whether a live‑action *Moana* can capture what made the original work. If the hair feels off, they wonder what that might imply about other design choices still to be revealed.

The Guardian article reflects this mood without claiming that any one reaction is universal. It reports that people are “wigging out” — reacting strongly and often humorously — but it does not present a single verdict on whether the wig is good or bad. Instead, it documents the fact of the debate and the kinds of arguments people are making.

## Why does this matter beyond social‑media jokes?

On the surface, a discussion about a celebrity’s hairpiece might seem trivial. The Guardian’s decision to cover the reaction, though, points to a few reasons the topic has resonance.

First, *Moana* is a major Disney property with a large, multigenerational audience. Changes to its characters, even small ones, attract attention because many viewers have a strong emotional connection to the original designs.

Second, Dwayne Johnson is one of the most recognizable actors in the world. Any significant change to his appearance for a role — especially something as visible as a full wig — becomes newsworthy in entertainment coverage. The Guardian’s piece uses that visibility to explore how audiences respond when a star’s look is reshaped to match a beloved animated figure.

Third, the reaction serves as an early barometer of audience expectations for the remake. While the Guardian report does not claim that the wig alone will determine the film’s success, it shows that viewers are already scrutinizing details and forming opinions long before release.
