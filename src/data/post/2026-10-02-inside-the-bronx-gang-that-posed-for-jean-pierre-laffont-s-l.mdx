On a Bronx street in the 1970s, a line of young men in denim vests and leather jackets stare straight into the camera. Some fold their arms; others rest hands on hips or pockets. Behind them, the brick walls are scarred and tagged. At their feet, a boy barely in his teens sits on the curb, looking up.

The photographer, Jean‑Pierre Laffont, later described them as “the most dangerous gang in the Bronx,” the Savage Skulls. The picture, recently revisited in an interview with the Guardian, has become one of his best‑known images of New York’s gang era.

## A gang, a street and a split‑second decision

Laffont told the Guardian that he had been reading stories at the time about gangs killing drug dealers who targeted young people. Those reports, he said, were on his mind when he encountered the Savage Skulls and decided to make the photograph.

He was working in the Bronx, documenting everyday life in a borough that was then synonymous in much of the US press with arson, disinvestment and violent crime. The Guardian recounts how he came across the gang on their own turf and asked to photograph them. The members agreed, arranging themselves in a loose formation that filled the frame.

In Laffont’s account, the danger was not abstract. The Guardian piece describes the Savage Skulls as a group that inspired fear locally, and notes his characterization of them as the Bronx’s most dangerous gang at the time. That description is presented as Laffont’s own assessment, grounded in what he had read and observed, rather than as a statistically proven ranking.

The result is a picture that feels both posed and volatile. The men’s stance suggests control; the tension in their faces suggests something less stable just beneath the surface.

## The Savage Skulls in Laffont’s frame

The Guardian’s interview focuses on this single image as a window into Laffont’s broader work. He had spent years photographing protest, poverty and political upheaval around the world. In New York, his lens often turned to people living at the margins of power.

In the Bronx photograph, the details do much of the storytelling. The Guardian notes the gang’s distinctive look: customized denim or leather vests, some with insignia, worn over T‑shirts or bare chests. The clothing marks them as a unit, but the faces are individual—some wary, some confrontational, some almost bored.

Laffont recalls, as reported by the Guardian, that he was struck by the presence of very young people around the gang. The boy on the curb, framed by older members behind him, anchors the lower part of the picture. To Laffont, that juxtaposition echoed the news stories he had read about gangs and drug dealers fighting over the bodies and futures of teenagers.

The Guardian article presents the photograph as one of Laffont’s most powerful precisely because it compresses those tensions into a single frame: youth and menace, community and threat, performance and reality.

## Fear, protection and the stories around gangs

Laffont’s remark to the Guardian about gangs killing drug‑pushers who targeted young people points to a complicated narrative that circulated about groups like the Savage Skulls in the 1970s. On one hand, they were widely portrayed in local and national media as violent organizations responsible for shootings, stabbings and turf wars. On the other, some reporting suggested that certain gangs claimed to protect their neighborhoods from outside dealers.

The Guardian’s account does not attempt to resolve that tension; instead, it situates Laffont’s photograph inside it. His image does not show drugs or weapons. It shows posture, proximity and the way a group chooses to present itself when a stranger with a camera arrives.

By highlighting that Laffont had been reading about gangs killing drug‑pushers, the Guardian underlines how those stories shaped his understanding of what he was seeing. His decision to photograph the Savage Skulls was not neutral; it was informed by fear, curiosity and a sense that this street‑corner encounter was part of a larger struggle over young lives in the Bronx.

The article treats his description of the gang as “the most dangerous” as a reflection of that climate, not as a verified ranking. Independent corroboration of that specific claim is limited in the material currently available, and the Guardian presents it as his own judgment rather than an official designation.

## Why the image still matters

Decades later, the Guardian’s decision to revisit the photograph underscores its staying power. Laffont’s picture has become part of the visual record of New York in the 1970s, a period often reduced in memory to burning buildings and crime statistics.

In the Guardian’s telling, what sets this image apart is its human scale. The Savage Skulls members are not anonymous silhouettes or distant figures in a police photo. They are close enough that viewers can see the creases in their jeans and the set of their jaws. The boy at the front is close enough that his expression can be read, and re‑read, by anyone looking.

For public understanding of that era, the photograph offers a counterpoint to more abstract accounts of gangs and crime. It shows who was actually on the street: young men and boys, living in a borough many wealthier New Yorkers rarely entered. It also shows how they wanted to be seen, at least in that moment—tough, unified, unafraid of the camera.

The Guardian’s profile of the image does not claim that it explains the full history of the Savage Skulls or of Bronx gangs more broadly. Instead, it treats the photograph as a fragment that invites questions: What futures awaited the people in the frame? How did the stories told about them—by journalists, police, neighbors—shape their lives and their neighborhoods?

Those questions remain largely unanswered in the material the Guardian presents. But the article makes clear that for Laffont, the photograph stands among his best because it crystallizes a time when the stakes felt immediate and deadly, especially for young people.

## What to watch as the story is revisited

As interest in archival photography grows, images like Laffont’s Bronx gang portrait are likely to be examined alongside new research and testimony about the communities they depict. The Guardian’s interview places Laffont’s own memories at the center, and its account is anchored in his perspective and the stories he read at the time.

For now, the photograph remains what it has long been: a stark, unsettling record of a group that, in Laffont’s words, was “the most dangerous gang in the Bronx,” and of the boy sitting at their feet. It matters because it forces viewers to look closely at the people behind the label “gang,” and to reckon with how images, stories and fear can shape the way entire neighborhoods are remembered.
