A small but growing labor movement is working to secure well‑paid union jobs in US clean energy projects, even as Donald Trump intensifies political attacks on the sector, according to recent reporting by the Guardian. The effort has drawn workers from traditional infrastructure roles into emerging industries such as offshore wind.

One of those workers is Ryan McElroen, who told the Guardian he spent years repairing New York City’s subway system before taking a job on an offshore wind project. After working in “darkness” underground, he described the new role as “polar opposite,” with work now “in the middle of the ocean.”

## From subway tunnels to offshore wind

McElroen’s move from the New York City subway to offshore wind illustrates how some union members are transitioning into clean energy roles. The Guardian reports that he had long worked underground on subway repairs before getting the opportunity to join a project located miles off the coast.

His description of the change in working conditions — from tunnels with no natural light to a job at sea with open views — underscores the concrete shift in the type of work available as new energy projects come online. While the Guardian account focuses on his experience, it presents his story as one example of a broader trend of union workers seeking stable, well‑paid positions in clean energy.

## Labor movement links climate and inequality

Since Hurricane Sandy struck the US East Coast in 2012, a segment of the labor movement has increasingly tied climate concerns to economic inequality, the Guardian reports. The storm, which caused widespread damage in New York and neighboring states, is described in that reporting as an early catalyst for unions and worker advocates to address climate risks alongside job security.

According to the Guardian, this post‑Sandy organizing has focused on ensuring that the transition away from fossil fuels includes “good jobs” — a term unions typically use to describe positions with living wages, benefits, and worker protections. The article portrays unions not only as reacting to climate impacts but also as trying to shape the terms of new energy investment.

## Trump’s attacks on clean energy

The Guardian reports that this organizing is unfolding against a backdrop of political attacks on clean energy from Donald Trump. In its account, Trump is described as targeting US clean energy projects, including offshore wind, in speeches and public remarks.

The same reporting frames these attacks as a direct challenge to the kind of projects now employing workers like McElroen. While the Guardian does not provide a full catalog of Trump’s statements in the passage referenced, it characterizes his stance as hostile to the expansion of clean energy and as a political headwind for unions trying to secure long‑term work in the sector.

Because the Guardian is the primary source for this description, and independent corroboration in this news cycle is limited, the precise scope and impact of Trump’s current interventions should be treated with some caution. The basic fact that he has criticized clean energy projects in public forums is well established in past coverage, but the Guardian’s framing of his latest attacks as a central obstacle to union efforts is based on its own reporting.

## What is at stake for workers

The Guardian’s account emphasizes that union advocates see clean energy projects as an opportunity to address both climate risks and long‑standing wage and inequality issues. In this framing, the stakes for workers include:

- Whether new offshore wind and other clean energy projects provide stable, union‑represented employment
- How political opposition, including Trump’s attacks, might slow or reshape those projects
- Whether lessons from disasters such as Hurricane Sandy translate into concrete protections and job standards

McElroen’s move offshore is presented as a tangible example of the kind of job that unions hope to multiply. His story, as reported, suggests that workers with experience in traditional public infrastructure can transfer their skills to new energy sectors if projects proceed at scale and under conditions acceptable to organized labor.

## Why this matters

The Guardian’s reporting links three developments: the long‑running recovery from Hurricane Sandy, a union push for better clean energy jobs, and Donald Trump’s current attacks on the sector. Together, they define a contested space where climate policy, labor rights, and energy investment intersect.

For readers, the key point is that unions are not only reacting to clean energy projects but actively trying to shape them into sources of secure employment. At the same time, Trump’s opposition, as described by the Guardian, adds political uncertainty around how many of those projects will move forward and under what terms.

Future developments to watch include how many workers follow paths similar to McElroen’s into offshore wind and whether union efforts can withstand continued political resistance to clean energy expansion.
