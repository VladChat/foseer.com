A sexually explicit commercial for a sports trading app featuring actress Sydney Sweeney has triggered a wave of criticism from female athletes, who say the ad reduces women in sport to sexualized props rather than competitors.

The backlash, reported across multiple outlets, centers on a spot in which Sweeney appears nude while promoting what is described as a sports-focused trading or betting platform. Critics argue the campaign leans on sexual imagery instead of athletic performance, sending a message that clashes with how women in sport want to be seen.

Australian surfer Felicity Palmateer, quoted in coverage of the dispute, summed up the frustration: “We want to be valued for what we can do, not what we look like.”

## How the ad sparked a backlash

An event-direct report on AlexJonesLive.com describes the commercial as a promotional spot for a sports trading app that features Sweeney nude, with the nudity framed as part of the marketing hook. The ad is positioned as a way to draw users into a platform that lets people trade or bet on sports outcomes.

NBC News and NBC Philadelphia, in their coverage of the same campaign, describe the reaction from female athletes who say the ad’s sexual tone clashes with ongoing efforts to secure equal recognition, pay, and media coverage. Their reporting notes that the criticism has come from athletes across different sports, including surfing, who see the commercial as sidelining athletic skill.

Across outlets, the core elements are consistent: Sweeney is presented nude in a high-gloss production for a sports-related trading or betting app; the creative concept is overtly sexual; and the reaction has been sharp from women who compete professionally.

## Athletes say the ad undercuts their work

NBC News reports that a number of female athletes publicly objected to the campaign, arguing that the ad reinforces a long-standing pattern in which women in sports are valued more for their appearance than for their achievements.

Palmateer, an Australian big-wave surfer, is cited as one of the most outspoken critics. In comments reported in coverage of the dispute, she said female athletes “want to be valued for what we can do, not what we look like,” framing the ad as a step backward from that goal. Her remarks have been widely repeated as capturing the central complaint.

Other athletes quoted in the broader coverage, according to NBC News and NBC Philadelphia, echoed similar themes: that the commercial’s focus on nudity and sex appeal risks drowning out years of work to build audiences around women’s performance, records, and rivalries rather than their bodies.

Breitbart’s account of the controversy notes that the ad has been labeled “sexualized” by critics and that the pushback is specifically coming from women who see themselves as part of the sports community the app is trying to reach.

While not every athlete has spoken publicly, the four independent outlets covering the story consistently describe a backlash led by women in sport who feel the campaign misrepresents how they want sports marketed.

## Sydney Sweeney’s response to the criticism

Breitbart and NBC Philadelphia both report that Sweeney responded to the controversy by leaning into the imagery rather than distancing herself from it. According to their coverage, Sweeney shared additional nude or semi-nude photos connected to the campaign on social media after the backlash began.

Those reports describe her posts as a kind of defiant response, signaling that she stands by the creative direction and is not retreating in the face of criticism. The outlets do not quote a detailed written statement from Sweeney, but they present her decision to share more images as her primary public reaction so far.

NBC News notes that the actress is a prominent Hollywood figure whose involvement likely helped the app gain attention quickly, both from fans and from critics. Her participation and subsequent response have kept the story in the news cycle longer than a typical ad backlash might last.

## Alex Jones enters the debate

AlexJonesLive.com, which provides the most direct event-focused coverage among the sources, frames the dispute in more overtly political terms and highlights the anger of female athletes. The site’s report also emphasizes that Alex Jones himself has weighed in, presenting his response as part of the story.

Based on that event-direct account, Jones addressed the controversy on his platform, responding to the uproar over the nude ad and the athletes’ complaints. The article characterizes his comments as a reaction to what he sees as a broader cultural fight over sexuality, sports, and media, though it does not provide an extensive transcript.

Because AlexJonesLive.com is the only source detailing Jones’s own remarks, and it offers only limited direct quotation, the exact wording and tone of his response remain partly unclear. What is consistent across coverage is that his involvement has added another layer of attention, pulling the story further into the culture-war arena and away from being solely a dispute between athletes, advertisers, and fans.

## What is at stake for women in sport

NBC News and NBC Philadelphia both situate the backlash within a longer-running debate over how women’s sports are marketed. Their coverage notes that female athletes have spent years pushing for more equitable coverage, arguing that media outlets and sponsors should highlight performance, competition, and records rather than sexualized images.

In that context, the Sweeney ad is seen by critics as a high-profile example of the old approach they have tried to move beyond. The concern, as described in those reports, is not only about one commercial but about the message it sends to sponsors and tech companies entering the sports space: that the fastest way to attract attention is to sexualize women.

Athletes quoted in the coverage say this risks undermining efforts to grow women’s sports as a serious commercial and cultural force. They argue that if new sports-adjacent technologies—like trading or betting apps—default to sexualized marketing, it could shape how fans, broadcasters, and investors perceive women’s competitions.

The four independent outlets covering the story all highlight that the dispute is specifically about sports and gender, not just about nudity in advertising more broadly. The repeated references to athletes, Sweeney, sports, and Sydney in their reporting underline that this is being treated as a sports-culture controversy rather than a generic celebrity scandal.

## What to watch next

In the coming days and weeks, attention is likely to focus on how the sports trading app and its partners respond to the backlash. None of the sources report a formal change to the campaign yet, but advertisers facing sustained criticism of this kind often consider editing, age-gating, or pulling ads if they judge that the damage to their brand outweighs the benefits.

Observers will also be watching whether more athletes or sports organizations weigh in. NBC News and NBC Philadelphia both suggest that the criticism so far has come from individual competitors; if leagues, teams, or sponsors publicly support or oppose the ad, that could influence how similar campaigns are designed in the future.

Finally, Sweeney’s next moves may shape how long the story remains in the spotlight. Breitbart and NBC Philadelphia report that she has so far doubled down by sharing additional images. If she or the app’s creators issue a more detailed statement—either defending the creative direction or acknowledging the athletes’ concerns—that could clarify how they see the balance between attention-grabbing marketing and respect for women in sport.
