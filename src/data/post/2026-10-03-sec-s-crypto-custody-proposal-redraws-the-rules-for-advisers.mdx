The U.S. Securities and Exchange Commission (SEC) has proposed a new framework governing how registered investment advisers and investment funds may hold cryptoassets, including conditions under which they could self‑custody or use state‑chartered trust companies. Reporting from Generation Impact, Reuters, Cryptopolitan, and Barron’s all describe the move as a formal attempt to set ground rules for “crypto custody” in the regulated investment industry, after several years of ad‑hoc guidance and high‑profile failures in the broader crypto market.

While the full rule text and its final shape are not yet settled, the proposal marks a significant step in defining when and how professional money managers can hold digital assets on behalf of clients.

## What the SEC Has Proposed

Generation Impact reports that the SEC has put forward a framework specifically addressing crypto custody by investment funds and advisers. Across the coverage, four independent outlets describe the same core development: the agency is proposing rules that would

- Define conditions for **crypto custody** by SEC‑registered investment advisers and funds
- Allow **conditional self‑custody** of crypto by advisers and funds
- Recognize **state trust companies** as potential qualified custodians for cryptoassets

Reuters characterizes the initiative as a set of rules for how investment advisers handle crypto holdings, aligning them with existing custody obligations for traditional assets. Cryptopolitan and Barron’s both emphasize that the proposal is framed as a **“framework”** rather than a one‑off exemption, suggesting the SEC is trying to systematize how digital assets fit into the existing custody regime.

The details of the conditions attached to self‑custody and state trust custody are not fully spelled out in the public summaries, and the rule remains at the proposal stage. That means changes are likely after a public comment period, and some elements may be tightened or softened before any final adoption.

## Why Crypto Custody Matters for Advisers and Funds

Under U.S. securities law, registered investment advisers that hold client assets generally must use a **“qualified custodian”**—typically a bank, broker‑dealer, or similar regulated institution—to safeguard those assets. This requirement is meant to reduce the risk of theft, loss, or misuse.

Cryptoassets have posed a challenge to that model. Many traditional custodians have been reluctant or slow to support digital assets, while unregulated or lightly regulated crypto platforms have failed or been hacked. The reporting across all four sources repeatedly highlights the terms **“investment,” “framework,” “proposes,” and “custody,”** underscoring that the SEC is targeting this specific structural gap rather than crypto trading more broadly.

By proposing a formal framework that includes conditional self‑custody and state trust company custody, the SEC is acknowledging that:

- Advisers and funds **already have exposure** to cryptoassets or are seeking it.
- The existing custody rule needs **clearer application** to digital assets.
- A purely prohibitive stance could push activity into **less regulated channels**.

In that sense, the proposal is not only a compliance exercise; it is also a gatekeeping decision about which types of institutions will be allowed to sit at the center of regulated crypto investing.

## How Conditional Self‑Custody Changes the Landscape

According to Generation Impact and Cryptopolitan, a notable feature of the proposal is that it would allow investment advisers and funds to **self‑custody** crypto, subject to conditions. The public summaries do not list every condition, but the framing implies that self‑custody would not be an unrestricted option.

From a structural standpoint, conditional self‑custody could:

- **Empower larger advisers and funds** that have the resources to build robust internal custody systems, including secure key management and audit controls.
- **Raise the bar for operational competence**, because advisers that choose this route would likely need to demonstrate strong safeguards to meet SEC expectations.
- **Narrow the role of lightly regulated third‑party crypto custodians**, if advisers conclude that either self‑custody or regulated trust company custody is preferable from a compliance standpoint.

The winners in this configuration are likely to be well‑capitalized firms capable of absorbing the cost and complexity of secure self‑custody. Smaller advisers, or those without deep technology and security teams, may find that the compliance burden makes self‑custody impractical, pushing them toward external custodians.

## State Trust Companies Move to the Forefront

All four sources point to **state trust companies** as a key part of the SEC’s proposed framework. Generation Impact and Barron’s note that the rules would allow advisers and funds to use state‑chartered trust companies as crypto custodians, subject to conditions.

This recognition matters because several crypto‑focused custodians in the U.S. operate as state‑chartered trust companies rather than as national banks or broker‑dealers. Under the proposed framework, these entities could become central infrastructure for regulated crypto investment, provided they meet whatever standards the SEC sets.

In practical terms:

- **State trust companies that already specialize in digital assets stand to gain**, as they are positioned to serve advisers and funds that do not want or cannot afford to self‑custody.
- **Traditional banks and broker‑dealers** that have been cautious about crypto may face more direct competition from specialized trust companies if advisers view them as compliant, crypto‑native options.
- **Advisers and funds** get a clearer pathway to use institutions that understand crypto operationally, without stepping outside a regulated custody framework.

However, the summaries do not clarify how the SEC will evaluate differences among state regimes. Some states have more permissive trust company rules than others. How the SEC interprets “qualified” in this context remains an open question and could materially affect which trust companies benefit.

## Who Wins, Who Loses Under the Proposal

Based on the consistent elements reported across Generation Impact, Reuters, Cryptopolitan, and Barron’s, the proposal appears to re‑draw the custody map rather than simply expanding access.

**Likely relative winners** (if the rule is finalized along the lines described):

- **Large registered investment advisers and funds** with the scale to implement secure self‑custody systems that satisfy SEC expectations.
- **Crypto‑specialist state trust companies** that can credibly position themselves as compliant custodians under the new framework.
- **Institutional investors seeking regulated crypto exposure**, who may gain more options for holding digital assets within familiar legal structures.

**Likely relative losers or pressured parties:**

- **Smaller advisers** that lack the resources for robust self‑custody and must navigate a narrower set of acceptable custodians.
- **Unregulated or offshore crypto platforms** that have historically acted as de facto custodians for some funds and advisers; the proposal, as described, points regulated advisers toward more formal custody channels.
- **Incumbent custodians that have delayed building crypto capabilities**, who may find that specialized trust companies capture early market share among advisers responding to the new rules.

These outcomes are interpretive, not yet observable facts. The actual distribution of winners and losers will depend on the final rule language, how strictly the SEC enforces it, and how quickly different institutions adapt.

## What Remains Unclear

The reporting agrees on the existence and broad contours of the proposal but leaves several important points unresolved:

- **Exact technical and operational standards** for self‑custody are not detailed in the summaries. It is unclear what level of security, segregation, and auditability the SEC will require.
- **How the SEC will treat different categories of cryptoassets** is not specified in the available coverage. The framework may or may not distinguish between tokens the SEC views as securities and other digital assets.
- **The status of various state trust regimes** remains ambiguous. The proposal acknowledges state trust companies, but the criteria for determining whether a particular state trust qualifies as an acceptable custodian are not fully described in the public reporting.

These gaps are typical at the proposal stage. The SEC often refines definitions and standards in response to public comments and further internal analysis.

## What Could Happen Next

Over the coming weeks and months, the proposal is expected to move through the SEC’s standard rulemaking process, including a public comment period. While the sources do not lay out a precise timeline, they all treat the framework as a proposal rather than a final rule, which means the outcome is not fixed.

A few plausible scenarios emerge from the current evidence:

1. **Tight but workable custody regime**: After comments from industry and investor advocates, the SEC could finalize rules that preserve conditional self‑custody and state trust company custody but with stringent security, reporting, and segregation requirements. In this scenario, large advisers and established trust companies likely consolidate their advantage, and smaller players adapt or exit.

2. **Narrowing of self‑custody options**: If feedback highlights significant risks or operational failures in proposed self‑custody models, the SEC may narrow or heavily condition self‑custody in the final rule, effectively steering most advisers toward third‑party qualified custodians. That would strengthen the position of regulated trust companies and traditional custodians that support crypto, while limiting experimentation by smaller advisers.

3. **Extended refinement and delay**: If comments reveal major disagreements over how to treat different cryptoassets or state trust regimes, the SEC could extend the process, issue revised proposals, or phase in requirements. That would prolong uncertainty for advisers and funds already holding crypto, but also give institutions more time to build compliant custody solutions.

Which path emerges will depend on the volume and content of public comments, the SEC’s internal risk assessments, and any new market events that highlight custody failures or successes. The direction of travel, however, is clear from the consistent reporting: crypto custody for regulated advisers and funds is moving from improvisation toward a formal, and likely more demanding, regulatory framework.
