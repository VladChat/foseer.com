Israel’s parliament has passed a law enabling courts to impose the death penalty on Palestinians convicted of lethal attacks, according to multiple news outlets that reported the vote within the past day. The measure marks a significant change in a country that has almost never used capital punishment since its founding.

Reporting by the Guardian and Reuters states that the new law targets Palestinians convicted of deadly offenses, with coverage describing the measure as focused on attacks resulting in deaths. Big News Network also characterizes the legislation as applying to Palestinians from the occupied West Bank convicted of lethal acts.

While the precise legal text and implementation details were not fully described in the initial coverage, all three outlets agree that the law introduces a death penalty option in cases involving Palestinians and fatal attacks, and that the move is a departure from Israel’s longstanding reluctance to use capital punishment.

## What the New Law Does

According to Reuters and the Guardian, the law allows Israeli courts to hand down death sentences to Palestinians convicted of lethal attacks. The reporting consistently links the measure to cases where a Palestinian has been found guilty of causing death.

The Guardian’s account frames the law as a measure that specifically opens the door to capital punishment for Palestinians convicted of deadly offenses, in contrast to Israel’s general criminal framework, which has not routinely included the death penalty. Big News Network similarly describes it as a death penalty bill for West Bank Palestinians convicted of lethal attacks.

None of the three sources provides a full legal clause-by-clause breakdown, so it is not yet clear from public reporting how the law defines qualifying offenses, what evidentiary standards apply beyond existing criminal rules, or whether the measure distinguishes between different types of lethal attacks. The coverage does, however, consistently emphasize that the law is designed to apply to Palestinians and that it is tied to convictions for causing death.

## A Rare Use of Capital Punishment in Israel

All three outlets note that Israel has rarely used the death penalty in practice. Reuters and the Guardian both highlight that capital punishment has historically been reserved for exceptional circumstances, and that the country has carried out very few executions since its establishment.

Although the articles do not provide a full historical list, they agree on the broader pattern: the death penalty has existed in Israeli law but has almost never been applied. The new legislation is therefore being reported as a significant policy shift because it explicitly authorizes death sentences in a defined category of cases involving Palestinians convicted of lethal attacks.

By emphasizing that Israel has “rarely used” capital punishment and has applied it only in “exceptional cases,” the coverage underscores how unusual it is for the Knesset to expand, rather than narrow, the circumstances under which a death sentence can be imposed.

## Political and Legal Context

The Guardian and Big News Network both describe the measure as controversial, particularly because it applies to Palestinians and is tied to lethal attacks. Their reporting indicates that the law has drawn attention due to its potential impact on Palestinians from the West Bank who are prosecuted in connection with deadly incidents.

Reuters’ account situates the law within Israel’s broader security environment, noting that the measure is framed by its backers as a response to lethal attacks. While the articles do not provide a full debate transcript from the Knesset, they make clear that the law passed and that it is specifically directed at Palestinians convicted of causing deaths.

## Why This Matters

The law is significant for two main reasons highlighted across the coverage. First, it introduces an explicit death penalty option in a country that, as Reuters and the Guardian emphasize, has almost never carried out executions and has historically treated capital punishment as an extreme exception.

Second, it is framed in all three reports as applying specifically to Palestinians convicted of lethal attacks, especially from the West Bank, which Big News Network notes directly. That focus raises questions about how the measure will interact with existing legal and military systems governing Palestinians and could shape how future lethal incidents are prosecuted.

Although the long-term consequences are not yet known, the law’s passage marks a clear policy departure that is likely to be closely watched by legal observers, Palestinian communities, and international actors concerned with the use of capital punishment and the conduct of security operations.
