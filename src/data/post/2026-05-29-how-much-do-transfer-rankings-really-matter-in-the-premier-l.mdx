Every Premier League season now ends with a familiar ritual: the definitive transfer ranking. This year, as reported by the New York Times, one such project re‑orders all 189 Premier League signings from worst to best for the 2025‑26 campaign, turning a year of recruitment into a single, sweeping table.

That kind of exercise is not just entertainment. It is a public verdict on how clubs spent hundreds of millions of pounds, which players justified their fees and which deals look like expensive misjudgments. With multiple outlets, including ESPN and the New York Times, running season‑end assessments of transfers and awards, these rankings have become a recurring part of the league’s information ecosystem.

The reader question is narrower: how likely is it that this specific re‑ranking of 189 transfers is formally confirmed in the next week? Based on the available reporting, the more useful way to think about that is not as a binary “confirmed or not,” but as a piece of editorial work that is already underway and is likely to be published and then debated, rather than ratified in any official sense.

## What is actually being reported?

The New York Times directly describes a project to re‑rank all 189 Premier League transfers from worst to best for the 2025‑26 season. That is the clearest, event‑level description in the available coverage and forms the spine of this analysis.

The article is framed as a comprehensive reassessment of every signing in the league, rather than a shortlist of hits and misses. It treats the transfer window as a closed data set: 189 incoming players, one season of evidence, and a retrospective judgment on where each deal sits on the spectrum from failure to success.

Two additional pieces of coverage provide context rather than new facts. ESPN’s season‑end awards for the 2025‑26 Premier League campaign include categories such as worst transfer and best XI, indicating that performance‑based evaluations of signings are a staple of post‑season analysis. A separate New York Times piece on Premier League predictions and fan expectations underscores how closely supporters and media track transfers as a proxy for club ambition and competence.

Across these sources, the same cluster of terms recurs: Premier League, transfers, season, awards, best and worst. That repetition supports the conclusion that a full‑scale re‑ranking of 189 transfers is a live editorial project tied to the close of the 2025‑26 season.

## Why a 189‑player ranking matters

On its face, ordering 189 transfers sounds like a niche exercise. In practice, such rankings sit at the intersection of sporting performance, financial scrutiny and fan politics.

From a performance standpoint, the ranking translates a season’s worth of matches into a comparative judgment: which signings improved their teams, which were neutral, and which actively harmed performance relative to cost and expectations. ESPN’s awards coverage shows that “worst transfer” has become as standard a category as “best player,” which tells us that failure is now catalogued as systematically as success.

Financially, every place on a 1‑to‑189 list is an implicit commentary on value for money. A high‑fee player who finishes in the bottom third of the ranking is, by definition, framed as a poor use of resources. Conversely, a low‑cost or free transfer near the top becomes a case study in smart recruitment. In a league where transfer spending is routinely scrutinized by fans and media, these rankings become a shorthand for who is “wasting” money.

For supporters, the list is a public ledger of promises kept or broken. Pre‑season coverage, like the New York Times’ predictions piece, often treats new signings as reasons for optimism. A season‑end re‑ranking closes that loop, revealing whether the optimism was justified. That feedback cycle shapes trust in club leadership.

## How such rankings are built

The sources do not set out the precise methodology behind the 189‑player ranking, so any description of the internal scoring system would be speculative. What can be inferred, based on how ESPN and the New York Times typically approach season‑end evaluations, is that several common ingredients are likely involved:

- **On‑field contribution:** Appearances, minutes, goals, assists, defensive actions and tactical fit are standard measures. Season awards coverage routinely cites these metrics when justifying best XI or worst‑transfer selections.
- **Expectations versus reality:** A marquee signing is judged against a higher bar than a squad player. Pre‑season predictions, such as those highlighted by the New York Times, help set that bar.
- **Cost and contract:** While not always quantified explicitly, transfer fee and wages usually inform whether a signing is described as good value or a flop.
- **Contextual factors:** Injuries, managerial changes or shifts in tactical systems can influence how harshly a transfer is judged.

Crucially, these rankings are editorial products, not official audits. They blend data with subjective weighting. That is why different outlets can arrive at different verdicts on the same player, even when looking at the same season.

## Who stands to gain or lose from the list

Although the ranking has no regulatory force, it can still shape perceptions in ways that matter to key stakeholders.

### Clubs and recruitment departments

Clubs whose recent signings cluster near the top of a 189‑player list can point to it as external validation of their recruitment strategy. In an environment where sporting directors and data departments increasingly compete for influence, a positive showing in widely read rankings strengthens the argument that their processes are working.

Conversely, a club with several big‑money signings rated near the bottom will face renewed questions about scouting, analytics and decision‑making. ESPN’s inclusion of “worst transfer” in its awards shows that these labels stick; they can become shorthand in future coverage, influencing how new signings are greeted and how ownership is judged.

### Players and agents

For players, being publicly tagged as one of the “worst” transfers can affect reputation, at least in the short term. While the available sources do not document specific career impacts, it is reasonable to say that such labels enter the conversation around future moves and contract negotiations.

Agents, meanwhile, can use a high ranking as evidence that a client exceeded expectations or offered exceptional value, especially if the player arrived on a modest fee. That narrative can be useful when pushing for improved terms or a move to a bigger club.

### Media and analytics firms

For outlets such as the New York Times and ESPN, comprehensive transfer rankings are a way to deepen engagement around the Premier League. They invite debate, drive subscriptions and reinforce the perception that these publications offer not just match reports but season‑long, data‑informed judgment.

Analytics firms that supply underlying data also benefit indirectly. As rankings become more granular and statistics‑driven, clubs and fans alike grow accustomed to treating performance data as central to understanding transfers.

## How “confirmation” works for an editorial ranking

The reader’s question about how likely this 189‑transfer re‑ranking is to be “formally confirmed” in the next week hints at a potential misunderstanding of what is being reported.

Unlike a player transfer, which is confirmed when clubs file paperwork and issue official statements, an editorial ranking is confirmed when it is published by the outlet that commissioned it. There is no governing body that ratifies a list of best and worst transfers.

The New York Times’ direct reporting that such a re‑ranking is being undertaken is itself a strong indicator that publication is planned as part of the post‑season package. ESPN’s parallel focus on awards and transfer outcomes reinforces that this is the moment in the calendar when such pieces typically appear.

Given that context, it is reasonable to expect that the ranking will be finalized and released in the near term, but there is no separate process by which it becomes “official.” Once the article is live, the ranking exists as that outlet’s considered view—subject to challenge, rebuttal and alternative lists from competitors.

## What to watch over the next few weeks

Over the coming weeks, several developments are plausible, though not guaranteed, based on the current reporting.

One scenario is that the New York Times publishes the full 1‑to‑189 ranking as a flagship piece in its Premier League wrap‑up coverage. If that happens, expect it to be heavily referenced in social media debates and possibly in follow‑up analysis by other outlets, much as ESPN’s awards pieces are cited when discussing season narratives.

A second scenario is that the ranking appears in a more modular form—broken into categories such as “biggest flops,” “best bargains” and “most improved.” That would align with the way ESPN structures its awards, focusing attention on discrete storylines rather than a single, linear table.

A third, less likely but possible outcome is that the project is delayed or scaled back, for example if editorial priorities shift or additional data and interviews are sought. In that case, the concept of re‑ranking 189 transfers would still shape how the season is discussed, even if the final product looks different from early descriptions.

In all cases, the key indicator to watch is straightforward: whether the New York Times publishes the promised re‑ranking as part of its 2025‑26 Premier League coverage. Once it does, the debate over who truly belongs at the top or bottom of that 189‑strong list will begin—and that debate, more than any formal stamp of approval, is what gives such rankings their power.
