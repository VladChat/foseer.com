Oman is resisting pressure from the United States to distance itself from Iran over security tensions in the Strait of Hormuz, according to reporting by the Guardian on Friday. The paper cites Omani officials who say Muscat will not break or downgrade relations with Tehran despite US efforts following recent Iranian attacks.

The stance underscores Oman's long‑standing role as a regional go‑between and highlights the limits of American leverage with one of its quieter Gulf partners. It also raises questions about how far Washington can push states that depend on working relations with Iran to keep vital sea lanes open.

## What Oman Is Refusing To Do

The Guardian reports that US officials have urged Oman to reduce or sever diplomatic ties with Iran in response to Iranian military actions that have heightened tensions around the Strait of Hormuz. The article does not specify the precise diplomatic steps Washington requested, but characterizes them as pressure to "break ties" with Tehran.

Omani officials quoted in the report say they have refused to take that step. Instead, Oman intends to maintain its existing relationship with Iran while continuing to coordinate closely with Western partners on maritime security.

This account currently rests on a single primary source. Independent corroboration of the specific US requests and Oman's internal deliberations remains limited and should be watched as more reporting emerges.

## Why the Strait of Hormuz Matters

The Guardian frames the dispute around the strategic importance of the Strait of Hormuz, the narrow waterway between Oman and Iran that connects the Persian Gulf to the Arabian Sea. A large share of the world's seaborne oil exports passes through this chokepoint.

Because of that traffic, any disruption or escalation there can have outsized economic and security consequences. Oman shares control of the strait's approaches and has historically worked with both Iran and Western navies to keep the route open. That practical role helps explain why Muscat is cautious about rupturing ties with Tehran.

## Oman's Stated Red Lines

According to the Guardian, Omani officials are signaling that their neutrality has limits. The report notes that Muscat has been highly critical of Israel's conduct in the current regional crisis, particularly what Oman views as disregard for international law.

At the same time, the Omani government issued a statement on Wednesday condemning the recent Iranian attacks, the Guardian reports. That dual posture—denouncing both Israeli actions and specific Iranian strikes—illustrates Oman's attempt to balance principles with its mediator role.

The public condemnation of Iran's attacks is a notable step for a government that usually prefers quiet diplomacy. It suggests Muscat is willing to criticize Tehran when it believes regional stability or legal norms are at risk, even as it rejects calls to cut formal ties.

## How Muscat Is Positioning Itself

The Guardian's account portrays Oman as trying to hold three positions at once: maintain dialogue with Iran, reassure Western partners, and signal disapproval of actions it sees as destabilizing. Officials quoted in the report argue that keeping channels open to Tehran is essential precisely because of the risks around the Strait of Hormuz.

From that perspective, breaking relations with Iran could reduce Oman's ability to defuse crises in the waterway it borders. The government appears to be betting that Washington ultimately values Oman's intermediary role more than a symbolic diplomatic downgrade.

The same report indicates that Muscat's criticism of Israel is sharper than its language on Iran, reflecting Oman's view of the current conflict and its legal dimensions. That imbalance could complicate Oman's efforts to present itself as an even‑handed broker, but it aligns with its public statements.

## Stakes for the US, Iran, and Israel

For the United States, the Guardian's reporting suggests a test of influence over a partner that hosts Western naval activity near one of the world's most sensitive shipping lanes. If Oman holds its line, it will underscore the limits of US efforts to build a tighter diplomatic front against Iran.

For Iran, Oman's refusal to cut ties preserves one of Tehran's few relatively stable relationships with a Gulf Arab state. That relationship has previously provided Tehran with a channel to Western governments on security and nuclear issues, according to past diplomatic accounts not detailed in the Guardian's latest report.

Israel is not a direct party to the Oman–Iran relationship, but the Guardian notes that Muscat is "highly critical" of what it sees as Israel's disdain for international law. That stance, combined with Oman's condemnation of Iranian attacks, positions Muscat as a vocal critic of both sides' conduct while still maintaining practical ties with Tehran.

## How Firm Is Oman's Resistance?

Based on the Guardian's reporting, Oman's current position is clear: it is resisting US pressure to break ties with Iran and has coupled that with a public rebuke of recent Iranian attacks. What is less clear, given the limited independent sourcing, is how much room Muscat has left itself to adjust its stance if the situation in the Strait of Hormuz worsens.

The same report does not describe any formal US ultimatum or timeline, nor does it detail specific economic or security consequences Oman might face for defying Washington's request. That absence of detail makes it harder to assess how intense the pressure is and how costly Oman's resistance could become.

Given the available evidence, a formal, public confirmation by Oman or the United States that Muscat has rejected a concrete US demand to break ties with Iran within the next week appears possible but not assured. The Guardian has already put this position on the record, citing Omani officials, yet neither side has moved to codify it in a joint statement or treaty‑level decision.

In diplomatic practice, such disagreements often remain in the realm of quiet exchanges rather than formal declarations. Without additional reporting from other outlets or official briefings, it is uncertain whether the underlying dispute will be elevated into a more explicit, on‑the‑record confrontation in the coming days.

## What to Watch Next

The key indicators to follow are straightforward. First, whether Oman's foreign ministry issues further statements on Iran or the Strait of Hormuz that either harden or soften its current line, relative to what the Guardian has reported. Second, whether US officials publicly reference Oman's role or signal displeasure with its stance.

Any new Iranian military activity in or near the strait could also force quicker decisions in Muscat and Washington. For now, based on the limited but specific reporting available, Oman appears determined to keep its diplomatic bridge to Tehran intact while trying to show it will not ignore actions that threaten regional stability.
