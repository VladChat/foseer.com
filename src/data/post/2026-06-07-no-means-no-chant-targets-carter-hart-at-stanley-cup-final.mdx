Fans at the Stanley Cup Final loudly chanted “No means no” at goalie Carter Hart, turning hockey’s biggest night into a pointed public response to off‑ice allegations that have already led to his suspension from the NHL.

The chant, reported by the New York Times, rang out one day after the league announced that Hart and four other players had been suspended for what the NHL called “deeply troubling and unacceptable” behavior, regardless of any eventual legal verdict.

## What Happened During the Game

According to the New York Times, the chant broke out while Hart was on the ice during the Stanley Cup Final, with a noticeable portion of the crowd joining in. The phrase “No means no” is commonly associated with sexual consent and has been used widely in campaigns against sexual assault.

The report describes the chant as clearly audible in the arena, cutting through the usual roar of a championship game. It was aimed directly at Hart, whose name was invoked in the coverage as the focus of the crowd’s attention.

While the specific minute of game time and the exact score at the moment of the chant were not detailed in the Times report, the incident occurred during live play, not during a stoppage or intermission, ensuring that both players and viewers in the arena could hear it.

## NHL Suspends Five Players Over Conduct

The New York Times reports that Hart is one of five players suspended by the NHL in connection with behavior the league described as “deeply troubling and unacceptable.” In its public statement, the league said the suspensions would stand “regardless of the verdict,” a phrase the Times highlighted as underscoring the NHL’s stance that its disciplinary decisions are not contingent solely on the outcome of any legal process.

The Times report does not list all five players by name in the cited passage, but it makes clear that Hart is among them and that the suspensions are tied to the same underlying incident or set of allegations.

By framing the behavior as “deeply troubling and unacceptable,” the league signaled that it viewed the matter as going beyond a routine disciplinary issue. The Times notes that the NHL’s language and the decision to suspend multiple players at once mark a serious response by the league.

## Fans Bring Allegations Into the Arena

The “No means no” chant, as documented by the New York Times, represents a moment where fan reaction explicitly linked the on‑ice spectacle to the off‑ice allegations.

The chant’s wording directly echoed the language commonly used in discussions of consent, suggesting that at least some fans were responding not just to the NHL’s disciplinary announcement but to the nature of the alleged behavior behind it. While the Times report does not quote individual fans, its description of the chant indicates that it was loud enough and sustained enough to be a notable part of the game atmosphere.

The article makes clear that the chant was directed at Hart personally, rather than being a general protest. That focus, combined with the timing—coming a day after the suspensions were announced—linked the crowd’s reaction to the league’s statement in a way that was visible to anyone watching in the arena.

## Why It Matters for the Stanley Cup Final

The New York Times positions the incident as significant because it brought a disciplinary controversy into the heart of the Stanley Cup Final, a series that typically centers almost exclusively on performance and legacy.

By chanting “No means no” at a suspended player, fans underscored that the story of this Final is not only about goals and saves but also about how the league and its community respond to serious allegations involving players.

The Times report notes that all five players, including Hart, were suspended before the league’s statement was issued publicly, meaning their status was already affecting team lineups and competitive dynamics. While the article does not quantify the exact impact on the series, it makes clear that the suspensions removed multiple players from contention at a critical moment in the season.

The chant, then, did more than express disapproval: it highlighted how off‑ice conduct and league discipline were shaping who appeared on the ice during the NHL’s showcase event.

## What to Watch Next

Based on the New York Times reporting, the key developments now center on three fronts: any further detail the NHL releases about the conduct behind the suspensions, any legal proceedings connected to the allegations, and how teams and fans continue to respond as the Stanley Cup Final progresses.

The league has already taken the unusual step, as the Times notes, of emphasizing that the suspensions will remain in place regardless of any eventual verdict. That stance suggests that the NHL views its internal standards as distinct from legal thresholds.

For now, the most visible public reaction inside the arena has been the “No means no” chant directed at Carter Hart. Whether similar responses emerge in future games, and whether the league or teams address fan reactions directly, will help determine how much this controversy continues to shape the experience of the Stanley Cup Final.
